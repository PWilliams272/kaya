Gym-to-Gym Grading Comparison

Climbing grades are subjective, and different gyms are known for "stiff" or "soft" grading. This tool finds users who have logged sends at multiple gyms and compares their highest sent grade at each gym. Since it's unlikely someone will send their hardest grade in only one or two visits, it is useful to increase the minimum logged days at each gym — but this comes at the expense of the total number of users available for analysis.

Data quality depends on the accuracy of user input. Some users misrepresent their height (note the break in the expected bell curve shape for Men at the 5'11"-to-6'0" threshold), or might choose not to record pieces of information.

Obvious outliers (e.g. |ape index| > 10 inches, where ape index is wingspan minus height) are removed from analyses involving those metrics. User Segmentation

Data are limited to successfully completed climbs, or "sends," that users actually log. Some users use the app to fully log all their climbing sessions, some only log sends they're proud of, and others try it once or twice and never come back. In all cases, we are blind to all failed climbing attempts.

Since the different logging characteristics have different downstream effects on our analysis, we first segment the user base. For now, we'll simply categorize them as "Active" or "Inactive" based on the total number of sends they've logged, total number of distinct climbing sessions they've logged, and their average number of sends per session.

A Bayesian Grading Model for Climbing Ability

Climbing grades are subjective — the same boulder problem might be called V5 at one gym and V6 at another. This project builds a hierarchical Bayesian model that separates a climber's underlying ability from gym-specific grading style, using nothing but the grades climbers already log in the Kaya app. It's an active investigation, not a finished paper — several sections below end with an open question rather than an answer, on purpose.

Overview

The model treats each climb attempt the way item-response theory treats a test question: a climber has some latent ability, a gym's grading has some latent softness or stiffness, and whether someone sends a given grade is a function of the gap between the two. Fit correctly, this lets you compare climbers and gyms on a common scale, purely from send logs — no route setter input, no manual calibration.

Three findings so far, in order of confidence:

  • Gym grading really does differ, and the model recovers a believable, tightly-estimated correction per gym once enough connected gyms are in the network (below).
  • Height has a real, non-linear effect on bouldering ability — not "taller is always better," but an optimal-height effect that peaks and falls off on both sides. This took five separate functional-form tests, including one that was flat wrong, to pin down with any confidence (full story below).
  • Climb-level grading correction doesn't work with this dataset — not from lack of trying, but because there usually aren't enough logged sends per climb to separate "this climb is mis-graded" from "this climber is having an off day."

Approach

Rather than fit the full model in one shot, it was built up in stages — start with the simplest possible version (per-user ability only), confirm it recovers sane estimates, then add one structural piece at a time (gym correction, covariates, reliability weighting), re-validating at each step. This staged approach caught several problems early that would have been much harder to diagnose in a single large model.

Two structural decisions worth calling out:

A "gap," not an equality

The naive version of this model says a climber's logged max grade is their ceiling. But nobody sends their absolute limit every session — the true ceiling is somewhere at or above the logged grade. The model represents this as a latent gap (how much harder the climber could plausibly go, drawn from an Exponential distribution scaled by how many times they've visited and how active a climber they are) linked softly to the structural ceiling, rather than forcing an exact equality. A hard equality constraint made the sampler fail outright — the geometry was intractable at this scale; the soft link fixed it.

Reliability-weighted visits

A climber who's visited a gym 20 times has had more chances to find their real ceiling than someone who's visited twice. The model accounts for this directly in the rate governing the gap latent, scaled by visit count and by a per-climber "sends per session" reliability signal.

Mathematical Framework

The full generative model, for a user \(u\) and gym \(g\). Heights and ape index are centered at their population medians (written with a tilde); user- and gym-level offsets use a non-centered parameterization throughout (each is a fixed-scale standard normal times a learned standard deviation) — this is what makes the sampler behave, not just a notational choice.

Ability

$$\text{ability}_u = \beta_0 + \beta_{\text{gender}} \cdot \text{gender}_u + f_{\text{height}}(h_u, \text{gender}_u) + \delta_1 \tilde{a}_u + \delta_2 \tilde{a}_u^2 + \epsilon_u$$
$$\epsilon_u = \sigma_{\text{user}} \cdot \tilde\epsilon_u, \qquad \tilde\epsilon_u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

\(\tilde a_u = a_u - \bar a\) is ape index (wingspan − height) centered at the population median \(\bar a\).

Height term — the final, settled form

A plain quadratic with a gender interaction, chosen after rejecting a more flexible bump function on visual/domain grounds (see The Height Investigation):

$$f_{\text{height}}(h, \text{gender}) = \gamma_1 \tilde h + \gamma_2 \tilde h^2 + \text{gender}\cdot\left(\gamma_1^{\times} \tilde h + \gamma_2^{\times} \tilde h^2\right)$$

\(\tilde h = h - \bar h\), height centered at the population median. \(\text{gender}\) is a continuous indicator (0 = male-coded, 1 = female-coded, 0.5 = ambiguous), so the interaction term scales smoothly rather than switching discretely.

Gym correction

$$\text{gym}_g = \sigma_{\text{gym}} \cdot \tilde\delta_g, \qquad \tilde\delta_g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Structural ceiling

A user's true best-possible grade at a given gym — ability shifted by that gym's grading style:

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g$$

Reliability-weighted rate

How far below their ceiling a climber's logged max grade is likely to sit depends on how many chances they've had to find it — visit count, plus a per-climber activity signal:

$$r_u = \frac{s_u}{\bar s} - 1$$
$$\lambda_{u,g} = \lambda_0 \left(1 + \kappa \cdot n_{u,g}\right)\left(1 + \rho \cdot r_u\right)$$

\(s_u\) = user's sends-per-session, \(\bar s\) = population median (so \(r_u\) is recentered at 0 rather than 1 — an earlier version centered at 1 and it collided with \(\lambda_0\)'s own scale, badly). \(n_{u,g}\) = number of visits by user \(u\) to gym \(g\).

The gap — not an equality

$$\text{gap}_{u,g} \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda_{u,g})$$

Instead of forcing the logged grade to equal the ceiling exactly (which made the sampler fail outright — an intractable geometry at this scale), the two are linked softly:

$$m_{u,g} + \text{gap}_{u,g} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(C_{u,g},\ \sigma_{\text{link}}\right)$$

\(m_{u,g}\) is the observed logged max grade. \(\sigma_{\text{link}}\) is fixed at 0.5, not estimated — found via a sweep; letting it float freely caused severe convergence failure despite deceptively low divergence counts.

Does the model's own output look like the real data?

A posterior predictive check — simulate grades from the fitted model and compare to what climbers actually logged — matched closely: mean grade 5.257 (real) vs. 5.258 (simulated), per-gym means within 0.01–0.03 of each other. One minor, understood artifact: the simulation has a thin, rare low tail the real data doesn't, from two independent noise sources occasionally compounding — invisible at the bulk-of-distribution scale, not a structural problem.

Who Climbs Harder?

With gym effects accounted for, the model estimates how much each personal covariate shifts a climber's ability. Ape index (wingspan minus height — a proxy for reach relative to body size) shows a clear, well-behaved positive relationship: more reach for your height helps, roughly quadratically over the observed range.

Gym grading style also comes out clearly once there's enough of a connected network to compare against — some gyms grade noticeably softer or stiffer than others, and the model recovers this as a per-gym correction with real statistical confidence.

The Height Investigation

Height turned out to be the hardest covariate to get right — worth walking through in full, including a mistake caught along the way, because the process is as informative as the result.

Final answer: zero for male-coded, real polynomial for female-coded Settled

The bump-function shapes below (kept for the record) didn't hold up to a plain visual sanity check — the gender-split version had left/right widths differing 8× (1.59in vs. 12.87in), which isn't an "optimal height," it's a cliff into a plateau. So the investigation was constrained to three options only: polynomial, linear, or zero — tested with the standard, unconstrained quadratic (γ1·h + γ2·h²) plus a gender interaction, no vertex reparameterization, no bump.

Result: male-coded climbers show no credible height effect at all — both γ1 and γ2 include zero. Female-coded climbers show a real quadratic effect — both terms credibly exclude zero (γ1=0.065 [0.033, 0.098], γ2=0.0033 [0.0007, 0.0057]). The parabola's vertex falls at ~58in — right at the bottom edge of the observed height range — so across almost the entire practical range this isn't a peak-in-the-middle bell at all, it's an essentially monotonic, mildly accelerating "taller is better" relationship for female-coded climbers, with no effect detected for male-coded ones.

This closes out the functional-form question — no further iteration planned. What follows is the investigation's history (coarse/fine splines, the vertex-quadratic, the bump function, its null-check, the gender-reconciliation test), kept for the methodological record since the process — including a shape that looked confident right up until a visual gut-check rejected it — is as informative as the final answer.

How we got here (superseded by the final answer above): five functional forms, one at a time

A plain quadratic (the obvious first choice) found essentially no curvature — flat, symmetric uncertainty around zero. That could mean "no real effect," or it could mean a quadratic simply can't represent a bounded optimal-height effect without either being too rigid or exploding at the extremes. Four more targeted tests followed.

f(h) = A · exp(−((h−peak)/width)²), with separate left/right widths so the falloff doesn't have to be symmetric. Amplitude came out at 0.437 [0.230, 0.658] — cleanly excluding zero — with the falloff itself asymmetric: being shorter than the peak costs ability noticeably faster than being taller does.

A methodological catch worth keeping in the record A piecewise-linear spline can only bend exactly at a pre-chosen knot — so its "peak at 71in" partly reflects where the knots were placed, not something the data discovered on its own. This was flagged before it got reported as a clean result, and is the reason the investigation moved to knot-free, structurally-bounded forms instead of trusting the spline's peak location at face value.

Stress-testing the peak: could this be an artifact?

A fair concern: everything above was "hunt for the shape assuming an effect exists." None of it tested whether these methods would report a fake peak on data with no real height effect at all — and the confidence level tracked how tightly each method constrained the shape (bump: confident, splines: borderline, vertex-quadratic: null), which is exactly the kind of pattern that can indicate a prior-driven artifact rather than a real effect.

Synthetic null check Passes

Simulated data through the model's own full generative pipeline — real users' actual heights, gym structure, and gap likelihood — with the true height effect set to exactly zero. Fit the identical bump model to it. Result: fitted amplitude −0.012 [−0.352, 0.479], correctly recovering the null, with peak becoming essentially unidentified — exactly the behavior you'd want when there's nothing for it to lock onto. The bump function does not have a structural bias toward manufacturing a fake peak.

Does the peak differ by gender?

An early spline-based test suggested yes — quite dramatically (66in for male-coded users, 76in for female-coded) — but that result rested on a 4-parameter interaction term fit against a thin female-tall tail, the same overfitting shape as a rejected ape-index spline elsewhere in this investigation. It needed re-testing with a method less prone to that failure mode.

Gender-shift bump function, reconciliation test Corroborated

Instead of a 4-parameter spline interaction, the bump's peak gets a single gender-scaled shift term — one extra parameter instead of four correlated ones, and much less prone to overfitting a small subgroup. Result: peak_shift = 8.71in [5.98, 11.48], credibly excluding zero. Male-coded peak: 64.0in. Female-coded peak: 72.7in. Different exact numbers from the spline (66/76in), but the same direction and a similar magnitude, now from an independent, lower-dimensional method.

Worth being honest that this cuts against intuition — a 10-inch-taller optimal height for female-coded climbers isn't the obvious prior to hold, and gender is inferred from first names via a probabilistic package, not self-reported, which adds its own noise. It's now corroborated by two independent methods rather than one, which is meaningfully more convincing than either alone, but it's not treated as closed.

Held-out predictive comparison Essentially tied

Split users 80/20 (not observations — held-out users get zero personalized signal from either fit, testing whether the height shape itself generalizes). Fit linear-height and bump-height models identically otherwise on the same train split, forward-simulated predicted grades for the 745 held-out users' 1,248 observations, compared to real logged grades.

linear MAE = 1.5011 vs. bump MAE = 1.4997 — bump wins on just 50.2% of observations, mean paired difference 0.0014 ± 0.0015 (smaller than 1 standard error). No detectable predictive improvement.

This doesn't contradict the credible-interval evidence above — it answers a different question. For a brand-new climber, most predictive error comes from not knowing their personal ability offset at all (σ ≈ 1.6 grade units, freshly drawn), which swamps a height effect of magnitude ≈ 0.4–0.9.

Why this history is superseded Everything above (peak locations, the bump function, its null check, the gender-shift reconciliation, the held-out MAE numbers) was built on the bump function's asymmetric-Gaussian shape — which turned out, on a plain visual check, to look nothing like a believable "optimal height" curve. The final answer section at the top of this page supersedes all of it: zero effect for male-coded climbers, a real quadratic (practically monotonic in range) effect for female-coded ones. One honest gap: the held-out predictive comparison above was run against the bump model, not this final quadratic — re-running it wasn't worth the added time given how small the quadratic effect is, but that means the "does this actually improve prediction" question is technically still open for the final model specifically.

Why Climb-Level Grading Correction Stays Out

The most natural extension of this model would add a per-climb correction — letting individual boulder problems be systematically harder or easier than their labeled grade, separate from the gym-level correction. Every reasonable version of this was tried, and every version failed the same way.

Three prior families, one consistent failure mode Settled — parked

Normal, StudentT, and a regularized horseshoe prior were all tried for the per-climb correction term. StudentT and horseshoe both looked genuinely promising on synthetic data — but once applied to real data with covariates included, all three collapsed the same way: the per-climb correction ate almost all the per-user ability signal instead of coexisting with it.

The first hypothesis was that covariates were confounding the climb correction. A control run with covariates removed entirely still failed the same way, which disproved that theory and pointed to something more basic: with roughly 1,625–18,499 climbs at a median of only 2–3 logged observations per climb, there usually isn't enough signal to separate "this climb is mis-graded" from ordinary per-user noise, regardless of prior family. Scaling from 2 gyms to the 6-gym network — more data, the one untested lever — didn't change this conclusion either.

Are Low-Send Climbs Mis-Graded?

A related question worth checking directly: climbs with very few logged sends could be low-sent because they're unattractive to climb, because they haven't been on the wall long, or because they're genuinely harder than their label suggests. Real per-climb send counts (grouped directly from send logs — a separate ascent_count field exists in the raw data but is confirmed unpopulated, always zero across all 136,761 checked rows) make this directly testable.

"Hasn't been up long enough": not supported, and mildly contradicted — low-send climbs have actually been observable longer (549 days median) than well-sent climbs (453 days), the opposite of what this hypothesis predicts.

"Genuinely harder than labeled" (proxy: do low-send climbs attract disproportionately experienced senders?): mixed. The pooled ratio is close to 1 (no systematic effect), though individual grade levels are noisy in both directions.

"Unattractive to climb": untestable with the data currently available — no route photos, ratings, or setter metadata exist to check against.

This doesn't change the climb-correction conclusion above — that failure mode is about sparsity itself, not about why the sparsity happens — but it's a useful piece of context for anyone extending this analysis later.

Open Questions

  • Reconcile "real" with "not predictively useful." The height effect is statistically real but doesn't improve held-out prediction accuracy (above). Worth deciding what role it should actually play in a deployed model — kept for interpretability/population-level analysis, or dropped in favor of the simpler linear term if prediction is the only goal.
  • EMG marginalization. The gap-likelihood construction (Exponential latent + Normal soft link) has a known closed-form marginalization (an Exponentially Modified Gaussian), which would fix the LOO reliability problem above. Scoped, not yet built.
  • Routes discipline. Everything above is bouldering only. Routes climbing has its own grading scale and dynamics — on the radar, intentionally deferred.
  • A known data gap. A past infrastructure outage left a multi-month gap in captured sends that won't self-heal through normal daily syncing. Not urgent, but will need a deliberate backfill at some point.
