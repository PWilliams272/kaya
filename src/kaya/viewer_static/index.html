Climbing grades are subjective, and different gyms are known for "stiff" or "soft" grading. This tool finds users who have logged sends at multiple gyms and compares their highest sent grade at each gym. Since it's unlikely someone will send their hardest grade in only one or two visits, it is useful to increase the minimum logged days at each gym — but this comes at the expense of the total number of users available for analysis.

User Segmentation

Data are limited to successfully completed climbs, or "sends," that users actually log. Some users use the app to fully log all their climbing sessions, some only log sends they're proud of, and others try it once or twice and never come back. In all cases, we are blind to all failed climbing attempts.

Since the different logging characteristics have different downstream effects on our analysis, we first segment the user base. A Bayesian Grading Model for Climbing Ability

Climbing grades are subjective — the same boulder problem might be called V5 at one gym and V6 at another. This project builds a hierarchical Bayesian model that separates a climber's underlying ability from gym-specific grading style, using nothing but the grades climbers already log in the Kaya app. It's an active investigation, not a finished paper — several sections below end with an open question rather than an answer, on purpose.

Overview

The model treats each climb attempt the way item-response theory treats a test question: a climber has some latent ability, a gym's grading has some latent softness or stiffness, and whether someone sends a given grade is a function of the gap between the two. Fit correctly, this lets you compare climbers and gyms on a common scale, purely from send logs — no route setter input, no manual calibration.

Three findings so far, in order of confidence:

  • Gym grading really does differ, and the model recovers a believable, tightly-estimated correction per gym once enough connected gyms are in the network (below).
  • Height has a real, non-linear effect on bouldering ability — not "taller is always better," but an optimal-height effect that peaks and falls off on both sides. This took five separate functional-form tests, including one that was flat wrong, to pin down with any confidence (full story below).
  • Climb-level grading correction doesn't work with this dataset — not from lack of trying, but because there usually aren't enough logged sends per climb to separate "this climb is mis-graded" from "this climber is having an off day."

Approach

Rather than fit the full model in one shot, it was built up in stages — start with the simplest possible version (per-user ability only), confirm it recovers sane estimates, then add one structural piece at a time (gym correction, covariates, reliability weighting), re-validating at each step. This staged approach caught several problems early that would have been much harder to diagnose in a single large model.

Two structural decisions worth calling out:

A "gap," not an equality

The naive version of this model says a climber's logged max grade is their ceiling. But nobody sends their absolute limit every session — the true ceiling is somewhere at or above the logged grade. The model represents this as a latent gap (how much harder the climber could plausibly go, drawn from an Exponential distribution scaled by how many times they've visited and how active a climber they are) linked softly to the structural ceiling, rather than forcing an exact equality. A hard equality constraint made the sampler fail outright — the geometry was intractable at this scale; the soft link fixed it.

Reliability-weighted visits

A climber who's visited a gym 20 times has had more chances to find their real ceiling than someone who's visited twice. The model accounts for this directly in the rate governing the gap latent, scaled by visit count and by a per-climber "sends per session" reliability signal.

Mathematical Framework

The full generative model, for a user \(u\) and gym \(g\). Heights and ape index are centered at their population medians (written with a tilde); user- and gym-level offsets use a non-centered parameterization throughout (each is a fixed-scale standard normal times a learned standard deviation) — this is what makes the sampler behave, not just a notational choice.

Ability

$$\text{ability}_u = \beta_0 + \beta_{\text{gender}} \cdot \text{gender}_u + f_{\text{height}}(h_u, \text{gender}_u) + \delta_1 \tilde{a}_u + \delta_2 \tilde{a}_u^2 + \epsilon_u$$
$$\epsilon_u = \sigma_{\text{user}} \cdot \tilde\epsilon_u, \qquad \tilde\epsilon_u \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

\(\tilde a_u = a_u - \bar a\) is ape index (wingspan − height) centered at the population median \(\bar a\).

Height term — the final, settled form

A plain quadratic with a gender interaction, chosen after rejecting a more flexible bump function on visual/domain grounds (see The Height Investigation):

$$f_{\text{height}}(h, \text{gender}) = \gamma_1 \tilde h + \gamma_2 \tilde h^2 + \text{gender}\cdot\left(\gamma_1^{\times} \tilde h + \gamma_2^{\times} \tilde h^2\right)$$

\(\tilde h = h - \bar h\), height centered at the population median. \(\text{gender}\) is a continuous indicator (0 = male, 1 = female, 0.5 = ambiguous), so the interaction term scales smoothly rather than switching discretely.

Gym correction

$$\text{gym}_g = \sigma_{\text{gym}} \cdot \tilde\delta_g, \qquad \tilde\delta_g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Structural ceiling

A user's true best-possible grade at a given gym — ability shifted by that gym's grading style:

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g$$

Reliability-weighted rate

How far below their ceiling a climber's logged max grade is likely to sit depends on how many chances they've had to find it — visit count, plus a per-climber activity signal:

$$r_u = \frac{s_u}{\bar s} - 1$$
$$\lambda_{u,g} = \lambda_0 \left(1 + \kappa \cdot n_{u,g}\right)\left(1 + \rho \cdot r_u\right)$$

\(s_u\) = user's sends-per-session, \(\bar s\) = population median (so \(r_u\) is recentered at 0 rather than 1 — an earlier version centered at 1 and it collided with \(\lambda_0\)'s own scale, badly). \(n_{u,g}\) = number of visits by user \(u\) to gym \(g\).

The gap — not an equality

$$\text{gap}_{u,g} \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda_{u,g})$$

Instead of forcing the logged grade to equal the ceiling exactly (which made the sampler fail outright — an intractable geometry at this scale), the two are linked softly:

$$m_{u,g} + \text{gap}_{u,g} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(C_{u,g},\ \sigma_{\text{link}}\right)$$

\(m_{u,g}\) is the observed logged max grade. \(\sigma_{\text{link}}\) is fixed at 0.5, not estimated — found via a sweep; letting it float freely caused severe convergence failure despite deceptively low divergence counts.

Does the model's own output look like the real data?

A posterior predictive check — simulate grades from the fitted model and compare to what climbers actually logged — matched closely: mean grade 5.257 (real) vs. 5.258 (simulated), per-gym means within 0.01–0.03 of each other. Who Climbs Harder?

With gym effects accounted for, the model estimates how much each personal covariate shifts a climber's ability. Ape index (wingspan minus height — a proxy for reach relative to body size) shows a clear, well-behaved positive relationship: more reach for your height helps, roughly quadratically over the observed range.

Gym grading style also comes out clearly once there's enough of a connected network to compare against — some gyms grade noticeably softer or stiffer than others, and the model recovers this as a per-gym correction with real statistical confidence.

The Height Investigation

Height turned out to be the hardest covariate to get right — worth walking through in full, including a mistake caught along the way, because the process is as informative as the result.

Final answer: zero for male users, real polynomial for female users Settled

The bump-function shapes below (kept for the record) didn't hold up to a plain visual sanity check — the gender-split version had left/right widths differing 8× (1.59in vs. 12.87in), which isn't an "optimal height," it's a cliff into a plateau. So the investigation was constrained to three options only: polynomial, linear, or zero — tested with the standard, unconstrained quadratic (γ1·h + γ2·h²) plus a gender interaction, no vertex reparameterization, no bump.

Result: male users show no credible height effect at all — both γ1 and γ2 include zero. Female users show a real quadratic effect — both terms credibly exclude zero (γ1=0.065 [0.033, 0.098], γ2=0.0033 [0.0007, 0.0057]). The parabola's vertex falls at ~58in — right at the bottom edge of the observed height range — so across almost the entire practical range this isn't a peak-in-the-middle bell at all, it's an essentially monotonic, mildly accelerating "taller is better" relationship for female users, with no effect detected for male users.

This closes out the functional-form question — no further iteration planned. What follows is the investigation's history (coarse/fine splines, the vertex-quadratic, the bump function, its null-check, the gender-reconciliation test), kept for the methodological record since the process — including a shape that looked confident right up until a visual gut-check rejected it — is as informative as the final answer.

How we got here (superseded by the final answer above): five functional forms, one at a time

A plain quadratic (the obvious first choice) found essentially no curvature — flat, symmetric uncertainty around zero. That could mean "no real effect," or it could mean a quadratic simply can't represent a bounded optimal-height effect without either being too rigid or exploding at the extremes. Four more targeted tests followed.

f(h) = A · exp(−((h−peak)/width)²), with separate left/right widths so the falloff doesn't have to be symmetric. Amplitude came out at 0.437 [0.230, 0.658] — cleanly excluding zero — with the falloff itself asymmetric: being shorter than the peak costs ability noticeably faster than being taller does.

A methodological catch worth keeping in the record A piecewise-linear spline can only bend exactly at a pre-chosen knot — so its "peak at 71in" partly reflects where the knots were placed, not something the data discovered on its own. This was flagged before it got reported as a clean result, and is the reason the investigation moved to knot-free, structurally-bounded forms instead of trusting the spline's peak location at face value.

Stress-testing the peak: could this be an artifact?

A fair concern: everything above was "hunt for the shape assuming an effect exists." None of it tested whether these methods would report a fake peak on data with no real height effect at all — and the confidence level tracked how tightly each method constrained the shape (bump: confident, splines: borderline, vertex-quadratic: null), which is exactly the kind of pattern that can indicate a prior-driven artifact rather than a real effect.

Synthetic null check Passes

Simulated data through the model's own full generative pipeline — real users' actual heights, gym structure, and gap likelihood — with the true height effect set to exactly zero. Fit the identical bump model to it. Result: fitted amplitude −0.012 [−0.352, 0.479], correctly recovering the null, with peak becoming essentially unidentified — exactly the behavior you'd want when there's nothing for it to lock onto. The bump function does not have a structural bias toward manufacturing a fake peak.

Does the peak differ by gender?

An early spline-based test suggested yes — quite dramatically (66in for male users, 76in for female users) — but that result rested on a 4-parameter interaction term fit against a thin female-tall tail, the same overfitting shape as a rejected ape-index spline elsewhere in this investigation. It needed re-testing with a method less prone to that failure mode.

Gender-shift bump function, reconciliation test Corroborated

Instead of a 4-parameter spline interaction, the bump's peak gets a single gender-scaled shift term — one extra parameter instead of four correlated ones, and much less prone to overfitting a small subgroup. Result: peak_shift = 8.71in [5.98, 11.48], credibly excluding zero. Male-user peak: 64.0in. Female-user peak: 72.7in. Different exact numbers from the spline (66/76in), but the same direction and a similar magnitude, now from an independent, lower-dimensional method.

Worth being honest that this cuts against intuition — a 10-inch-taller optimal height for female users isn't the obvious prior to hold, and gender is inferred from first names via a probabilistic package, not self-reported, which adds its own noise. It's now corroborated by two independent methods rather than one, which is meaningfully more convincing than either alone, but it's not treated as closed.

Held-out predictive comparison Essentially tied

Split users 80/20 (not observations — held-out users get zero personalized signal from either fit, testing whether the height shape itself generalizes). Fit linear-height and bump-height models identically otherwise on the same train split, forward-simulated predicted grades for the 745 held-out users' 1,248 observations, compared to real logged grades.

linear MAE = 1.5011 vs. bump MAE = 1.4997 — bump wins on just 50.2% of observations, mean paired difference 0.0014 ± 0.0015 (smaller than 1 standard error). No detectable predictive improvement.

This doesn't contradict the credible-interval evidence above — it answers a different question. For a brand-new climber, most predictive error comes from not knowing their personal ability offset at all (σ ≈ 1.6 grade units, freshly drawn), which swamps a height effect of magnitude ≈ 0.4–0.9.

Why this history is superseded Everything above (peak locations, the bump function, its null check, the gender-shift reconciliation, the held-out MAE numbers) was built on the bump function's asymmetric-Gaussian shape — which turned out, on a plain visual check, to look nothing like a believable "optimal height" curve. The final answer section at the top of this page supersedes all of it: zero effect for male users, a real quadratic (practically monotonic in range) effect for female users. One honest gap: the held-out predictive comparison above was run against the bump model, not this final quadratic — re-running it wasn't worth the added time given how small the quadratic effect is, but that means the "does this actually improve prediction" question is technically still open for the final model specifically.

Why Climb-Level Grading Correction Stays Out

The most natural extension of this model would add a per-climb correction — letting individual boulder problems be systematically harder or easier than their labeled grade, separate from the gym-level correction. Every reasonable version of this was tried, and every version failed the same way.

Three prior families, one consistent failure mode Settled — parked

Normal, StudentT, and a regularized horseshoe prior were all tried for the per-climb correction term. StudentT and horseshoe both looked genuinely promising on synthetic data — but once applied to real data with covariates included, all three collapsed the same way: the per-climb correction ate almost all the per-user ability signal instead of coexisting with it.

The first hypothesis was that covariates were confounding the climb correction. A control run with covariates removed entirely still failed the same way, which disproved that theory and pointed to something more basic: with roughly 1,625–18,499 climbs at a median of only 2–3 logged observations per climb, there usually isn't enough signal to separate "this climb is mis-graded" from ordinary per-user noise, regardless of prior family. Scaling from 2 gyms to the 6-gym network — more data, the one untested lever — didn't change this conclusion either.

Are Low-Send Climbs Mis-Graded?

A related question worth checking directly: climbs with very few logged sends could be low-sent because they're unattractive to climb, because they haven't been on the wall long, or because they're genuinely harder than their label suggests. Real per-climb send counts (grouped directly from send logs — a separate ascent_count field exists in the raw data but is confirmed unpopulated, always zero across all 136,761 checked rows) make this directly testable.

"Hasn't been up long enough": not supported, and mildly contradicted — low-send climbs have actually been observable longer (549 days median) than well-sent climbs (453 days), the opposite of what this hypothesis predicts.

This doesn't change the climb-correction conclusion above — that failure mode is about sparsity itself, not about why the sparsity happens — but it's a useful piece of context for anyone extending this analysis later.

Open Questions

  • Reconcile "real" with "not predictively useful." The height effect is statistically real but doesn't improve held-out prediction accuracy (above). Worth deciding what role it should actually play in a deployed model — kept for interpretability/population-level analysis, or dropped in favor of the simpler linear term if prediction is the only goal.
  • EMG marginalization. The gap-likelihood construction (Exponential latent + Normal soft link) has a known closed-form marginalization (an Exponentially Modified Gaussian), which would fix the LOO reliability problem above. Scoped, not yet built.
  • Routes discipline. Everything above is bouldering only. Routes climbing has its own grading scale and dynamics — on the radar, intentionally deferred.
  • A known data gap. A past infrastructure outage left a multi-month gap in captured sends that won't self-heal through normal daily syncing. Not urgent, but will need a deliberate backfill at some point.

The Headline

Across a connected network of 29 gyms, the stiffest grader and the softest differ by 1.26 grades — more than a full V-grade. Twenty of the 29 differ from the network average by a margin that excludes zero.

Why this is bigger than the previous estimate. Version 1 measured six gyms that all belong to Touchstone. Those six span only 0.25 grades between them — they are a single company with a shared setting culture, so what v1 really measured was within-brand consistency, not gym-to-gym variation. The full network spans 4.9× wider.

How Climbers Improve Over Time

This section comes early because its answer is an input to the model that follows, not a commentary on it. The model treats a climber as a fixed quantity. They are not. Its ceiling for climber \(u\) at gym \(g\) is

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g$$

and there is no \(t\) anywhere in it. So if someone logged at one gym in 2022 and another in 2025, three years of getting better is charged to the second gym. The model has no other place to put it. A gym whose climbers reached it late in their own careers will look stiffer than it is; one they passed through early will look softer.

This section asks two questions in order, because the second cannot be answered without the first: how fast do climbers actually improve, and is the gym result contaminated?

How fast do climbers advance?

How this is measured

Each climber’s sends are bucketed into 90-day windows. A window with at least 5 sends gets a level: the hardest grade sent in it. Windowed, not cumulative — a running max-so-far only ever goes up, which would manufacture improvement out of noise. A windowed max can fall.

Consecutive windows up to 15 months apart then give a rate in grades per year. Both estimators use the same windows and the same rate; they differ only in which window decides the grade bin.

The obvious way to measure this is to take each climber’s level now, their level later, and bin the difference by where they started:

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\ell_{2} - \ell_{1}}{\Delta t}\ ,\qquad \text{binned by }\ \ell_{1}$$

This is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that produces a very convincing answer. Because \(\ell_1\) is a maximum over a handful of sends, a lucky window inflates it — and the next window, being ordinary, looks like a decline. The same noise that puts a climber in a high bin pushes their measured change downward. The estimator tilts the curve by construction.

How you can tell it is broken. The naive estimator says V11 climbers lose 3.6 grades a year. Nobody does that. When an estimator returns a physically impossible number at one end, the whole curve is suspect — not just that bin.

The fix: stop asking one measurement to do two jobs

Look at what \(\ell_1\) is being used for above. It decides which grade bin the climber goes in, and it is also the starting point that gets subtracted. One measurement, two jobs — and a single piece of luck hits both at once, in opposite directions:

  • A climber has a great week, so \(\ell_1\) comes out a grade too high.
  • Job one files them a grade too high.
  • Job two makes \(\ell_2 - \ell_1\) come out a grade too low.

So the top bins are systematically stocked with climbers whose starting point was inflated, and an inflated starting point mechanically produces a negative change. You get “strong climbers decline” out of data where nobody’s ability moved at all.

So hand job one to a different window. Use three windows instead of two: \(w_0\) only decides the bin, and the change is measured between \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) — windows \(w_0\) had nothing to do with.

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\ell_{w_2} - \ell_{w_1}}{\Delta t}\ ,\qquad \text{binned by }\ \ell_{w_0}$$

Now the luck that landed someone in the V8 bin happened in a window that is not part of the subtraction. It cannot tilt the measured change either way. The wire between the two errors is cut.

The same trap outside climbing. Suppose you want to know whether shorter children grow faster, and your tape measure is sloppy. If one measurement both decides who counts as short and serves as the baseline you subtract, then every child accidentally measured too short is filed as short and appears to grow extra. You will “discover” that short children grow faster — with a clean, monotone curve — even if every child grows at exactly the same rate. Measure on Monday to decide who is short, measure growth from Tuesday to Friday, and Monday’s slop cannot manufacture Tuesday-to-Friday growth.

Two costs, and the important one runs in the safe direction. Every climber now needs three good windows instead of two, so 20,756 triples replace 30,132 pairs. And because the bin label \(\ell_{w_0}\) is itself noisy, climbers get sorted into slightly wrong bins, which flattens any real relationship. So the de-biased slope is more likely an understatement of the grade dependence than an overstatement — which is the direction to err in when the claim being made is that advancement slows down.

A small second bug: dividing by a wobbly number

Nothing above pins down \(\Delta t\). \(w_2\) is just “the next window with enough sends”, so every climber contributes a change measured over their own gap — median 3 months, but some barely over one. And \(\ell\) is a max over integer grades, so nearly every gap produces a change of exactly \(0\) or \(\pm 1\). Divide a \(+1\) by a gap of \(0.09\) years and that climber enters the average at +11 grades a year.

$$\overline{\left(\frac{\Delta \ell}{\Delta t}\right)} \;\neq\; \frac{\overline{\Delta \ell}}{\overline{\Delta t}}$$

The fix is to divide once, at the end: average the changes, average the elapsed times, then take the ratio. It is worth doing and it is nearly free — but it is worth being clear that it barely moves the answer. The floor at \(\Delta t > 0.08\) years was already excluding the worst offenders, and the correction turns out to be around 0.05 grades a year. Which raises the real question, since the choice of measurement window moves the low grades by four times that much.

The real problem: how long do you watch?

Nothing so far says how long \(\Delta t\) should be, and that turns out to matter far more than the arithmetic. Hold everything else fixed and change only the watching period:

At V1 the answer moves from +0.88 grades a year to +0.22 depending on nothing but how long you look. The high bins barely move at all. A number that swings by a factor of four on an arbitrary choice is not a measurement of anything, so the question is which one — if either — is right.

Neither. A rate has to compound, and this one does not. Saying V1 climbers improve at 0.88 grades a year is a prediction: watch for a year and they should gain 0.88 grades. They gain 0.22, which is 4.8 standard errors away. The V1 group gains about a fifth of a grade after three months — and still about a fifth of a grade after twelve. That is a one-time step, not a rate, and dividing a step by the window you happened to pick just reports your choice back to you. Divide it by 0.25 and it reads +0.88; divide the same step by 1.0 and it reads +0.22.

So stop picking a window. Use them all: a genuine rate means the gain grows in proportion to elapsed time, so fit that line through the origin across all six horizons at once, and let the fit report whether the proportionality actually holds.

$$\overline{\Delta \ell}(h) = f(v)\cdot h \ ,\qquad \hat f(v) = \frac{\sum_h w_h\, h\, \overline{\Delta \ell}(h)} {\sum_h w_h\, h^2}\ ,\qquad w_h = 1/\mathrm{SE}_h^2$$

This is the one estimator that can be wrong in a way you can see. If a bin were really a step rather than a rate, the points would not lie on a line through the origin and the \(\chi^2\) would say so. It comes out near one in almost every bin — including V1, once the inflated three-month point stops being taken on its own.

The bin labels are lying, and always toward the middle. \(\ell_{w_0}\) is itself a noisy max, so each bin is stocked with climbers whose real level sits closer to the population average. Measured at the start of the window, the “V1” bin averages V2.0 and the “V9” bin averages V8.6. The x-axis is compressed by about a grade at each end, so every slope below is an underestimate of the real grade dependence — the safe direction to err when the claim is that advancement slows down.

Pooled across the bulk of the data, that line is about as clean as this kind of measurement gets. V3–V8 together, the gain accumulates in proportion to elapsed time from three months out to two years — so for most climbers improvement really is a steady rate, and the curve below is about where you are rather than how long anyone looked.

Is the gym result contaminated?

Now the question that motivated it. For every \((u,g)\) row, take the date of that climber’s hardest send at that gym and centre it within the climber:

$$\tilde t_{u,g} = t_{u,g} - \bar t_u \qquad \text{where } \bar t_u = \frac{1}{|G_u|}\sum_{g' \in G_u} t_{u,g'}$$

Within-climber centring is what turns this from a description into a test. It does not ask when a gym’s climbers were active; it asks whether this gym’s row is late for this person — precisely the quantity an unmodelled improvement trend would smuggle into the gym correction. How this goes into the model

The obvious repair is one parameter: a population improvement rate \(\tau\) applied to the centred date.

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g + \tau\,\tilde t_{u,g}$$

One number, no new geometry, and it absorbs exactly the “this climber’s row here is two years later than their row there” effect. But the advancement curve above says a constant is the wrong shape. Improvement is grade-dependent — around a fifth of a grade a year at V3, essentially flat by V7 — so a single \(\tau\) would over-correct strong climbers and under-correct weak ones. The rate has to be a function of where the climber already is, and the section above is exactly that function. It is used directly, interpolating between the measured bins. A straight line through them is a fair description — the fitted curve runs close to \(0.34 - 0.035\,v\) — but interpolation costs nothing and does not have to be defended.

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g + f\!\left(\bar m_u\right)\tilde t_{u,g}\ ,\qquad f = \text{interp}\big(\{(v,\ \hat f_v)\}\big)$$

The decisive choice is whether \(f\) is fitted or supplied. It is supplied — fixed at the measured values, contributing a known offset with no free parameter. The reason is that two different mechanisms produce the same correlation, and nothing in this data separates them:

  • Climbers improved between their two gyms. That is the curve measured above.
  • Gyms’ grading drifted — setters across the network got stiffer over the same period.

A fitted \(\tau\) would inflate to absorb whichever is larger and then charge all of it to climber improvement, soaking up real gym signal and biasing the corrections toward each other. A fixed offset can only remove the amount improvement actually justifies. It is the conservative direction, and the one that survives being questioned.

The tell that this matters. The observed date-to-correction slope is +0.55 grades per year, while measured advancement where the median climber sits is +0.10. If improvement were the whole story those two numbers would agree. They differ by roughly 5×, so most of the correlation is not climbers getting better — and a free \(\tau\) would land near that ratio and report a false explanation with confident-looking error bars.

Expect the correction to be small. The gyms span 0.90 years of centred date, so at the measured rate the offset moves the corrections by about 0.09 grades against an observed spread of 1.29 — a few percent. It is worth carrying because it is comparable to the gym-correction standard errors and can reorder gyms that sit close together, not because it rescues the headline. If it moved things by half a grade, that would be evidence the fixed curve is wrong rather than evidence something had been repaired.

The fixed version is also insensitive to binning. \(f\) runs from 0.25 at V3 to 0.04 at V9 — the whole span is a fifth of a grade per year, and across V4–V8, where the great majority of modelled climbers sit, it varies by half that. Misfiling someone by a grade changes their offset by hundredths, which matters because the bin labels are known to be off by about that much.

Still open, in order.

1. Carry the date through. An observation is a (climber, gym) pair — the hardest send there — so the date needed is that send’s, centred within climber. prepare_base_data currently reads the send date, uses it for visit counts, and drops it.

2. Report the shift, not a new headline. Keep the no-offset fit as the comparison arm and quote how far the corrections move.

3. Fit \(\tau\) once, as a diagnostic. Not to ship — to measure. If the fitted rate lands near the ratio in the box above rather than near the measured curve, that is evidence for gym-side grading drift, which deserves its own section rather than being quietly corrected away.

The Model, Formally

An item-response-theory model: climbs are test questions, climbers are test-takers, a send is a correct answer. The wrinkle is that Kaya records only sends — never failed attempts — so the standard IRT likelihood is not identified. Everything below follows from working around that one missing piece of data.

Ability

Each climber \(u\) has a latent ability on the grade scale:

$$\text{ability}_u = \beta_0 + \mathbf{x}_u^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \sigma_{\text{user}}\,\tilde\epsilon_u, \qquad \tilde\epsilon_u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

where \(\mathbf{x}_u\) is the covariate row for that climber — gender, height terms, ape-index terms, and two missingness indicators — each column centred to mean zero, which makes \(\beta_0\) orthogonal to every slope.

Height and ape index

Height and ape index are near-independent here (\(r = +0.137\)), so their shapes are specified separately. \(\tilde h\) and \(\tilde a\) are centred at the median and scaled to standard deviations.

The gender-interaction form is easiest to read as what it actually is — one curve per gender, blended. \(G\) is the probability the climber is female, so \(1-G\) and \(G\) are the weights:

$$f_{\text{height}}(\tilde h,\ G) \;=\; \underbrace{(1-G)\left(\gamma_1^{M}\tilde h + \gamma_2^{M}\tilde h^{2}\right)}_{\text{male users}} \;+\; \underbrace{G\left(\gamma_1^{F}\tilde h + \gamma_2^{F}\tilde h^{2}\right)}_{\text{female users}}$$

Note that \(G\) is a probability, not a hard label. A climber whose name is 70% female contributes 0.7 of a row to the female curve and 0.3 to the male one, which is how uncertainty about the name propagates into the height estimate instead of being silently rounded away.

What is actually fitted. The sampler does not see \(\gamma^{M}\) and \(\gamma^{F}\) as two free pairs. It fits a baseline and a difference — \(\gamma^{M}_{k}=\gamma_k\) and \(\gamma^{F}_{k}=\gamma_k+\gamma_k^{\times}\) — which is the same function, but lets the prior sit on the thing under test. A tight \(\gamma_k^{\times}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,\text{small})\) shrinks the two curves toward each other, so a gender difference has to be earned from the data rather than appearing by default. Fitting two independent pairs would impose no such discipline. The male/female form above is the same model, written for reading.
$$f_{\text{ape}}(\tilde a) \;=\; \delta_1\tilde a + \delta_2\tilde a^{2}$$

Six candidate height shapes are compared by LOO on identical data: the gender-blended quadratic above, a plain quadratic, linear, zero, a monotone saturating form — "reach helps until you have enough of it, then stops paying" — and a vertex quadratic that estimates the best height directly:

$$f_{\text{sat}}(\tilde h) \;=\; A\,\operatorname{logistic}\!\left(\frac{\tilde h - h_0}{s}\right) \qquad f_{\text{vtx}}(\tilde h) \;=\; -\kappa_h\left(\tilde h - p\right)^{2}$$

Ability, grades, and gym corrections all live on the same scale — V-grades — which is what makes a correction of “+0.4” readable as “four tenths of a grade stiffer.” Every symbol above and below is defined in the Symbols panel, docked to the right so it stays in view while you read. In every card the left band is where female users sit (64.2 ± 2.8 in) and the right band is male users (69.2 ± 3.3 in), each one standard deviation wide. All six cards share a vertical scale so their shapes are directly comparable; drag a slider to an extreme and the axis grows rather than clipping the curve.

Does centring height at the median force the peak to sit there? No — worth being explicit about, because it looks like it should. Centring only fixes where \(\tilde h=0\) falls; it says nothing about where the curve turns over. For \(f(\tilde h)=\gamma_1\tilde h+\gamma_2\tilde h^2\) the vertex sits at \(\tilde h^{*}=-\gamma_1/2\gamma_2\), which the data are free to put anywhere — v1’s own fitted coefficients implied a peak roughly 10 inches below the median.

What centring does change is the implied prior on that peak. A ratio of two normals is Cauchy-like — heavy-tailed and piled up near zero — so the plain quadratic quietly favours a peak near the median, and gives a badly-behaved interval whenever \(\gamma_2\) is close to zero. The vertex quadratic makes the peak \(p\) a parameter with a prior of its own, so it reports a credible interval directly instead of propagating error through a division. Both forms are fitted; if they disagree about where the best height is, that disagreement is itself the finding.

Gym correction

The model is identified only up to an additive shift between ability and correction, so corrections are constrained to sum to zero exactly — "relative to the average gym" by construction:

$$\text{gym}_g = \sigma_{\text{gym}}\,\tilde\delta_g, \qquad \tilde\delta \sim \text{ZeroSumNormal}(0,1)$$
$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g$$

The gap, and the likelihood

Nobody sends their true limit every session, so the logged max sits at or below the ceiling by a non-negative gap, whose rate rises with how many chances the climber has had to find their limit:

$$\log \lambda_{u,g} = \log\lambda_0 + \kappa\,\tilde n_{u,g} + \rho\,\tilde r_u$$
$$\text{gap}_{u,g}\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda_{u,g}), \qquad m_{u,g} = C_{u,g} - \text{gap}_{u,g} + \varepsilon,\ \ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{link}})$$

A Normal minus an Exponential is an Exponentially Modified Gaussian, so the gap integrates out in closed form and never has to be sampled:

$$-m_{u,g} \;\sim\; \text{ExGaussian}\!\left(-C_{u,g},\ \sigma_{\text{link}},\ 1/\lambda_{u,g}\right)$$

That removes one latent variable per observation — 32,501 of them on this network — and turns the likelihood into an ordinary observed node, so LOO and posterior predictive checks work natively. Verified against numerical integration of the original formulation to 1×10−8.

How to read a trace plot

Four chains start from different random points and wander around the posterior. Each line is one chain’s value for the parameter at each draw. What you are looking for is boring:

what you seewhat it means
Fuzzy caterpillar — dense, even, no pattern Good. The chain is bouncing around the same region draw after draw, which is what sampling a settled distribution looks like.
All four lines on top of each other Good. Chains that started in different places agree about where the answer is. This is what R̂ measures numerically.
Chains on visibly different levels (watch the dashed per-chain means) Bad. They disagree. The posterior mean is then an average over chains that never reconciled — this is R̂ above 1.01.
Slow drift or a trend across the plot Bad. Still moving, so it has not settled. Needs longer warm-up or a better parameterisation.
Flat stretches where a line sticks Bad. Proposals are being rejected repeatedly; the sampler is stuck against a difficult bit of geometry.
Wide excursions to far-off values Usually heavy tails, sometimes a second mode. Worth checking against the density plot beside it.

Where the warm-up went. PyMC discards tuning draws by default, so for the earlier fits the 600 warm-up iterations were thrown away before the trace was written and cannot be recovered. Fits run after this was noticed keep theirs, and show it shaded at the left with x = 0 marking the handover. Warm-up is where the dramatic convergence happens — chains starting far apart and pulling together — which is exactly why it is worth keeping.

The rank plot option asks the same question a different way, useful because good traces all look alike and the eye is bad at judging four overlapping squiggles. Pool every draw from every chain, sort them, and ask where each chain’s draws landed in that ordering. If the chains explored the same distribution, each contributes evenly across the whole range and its bars sit flat on the dashed line. A chain parked somewhere the others never went piles up at one end. Strictly a diagnostic — ignore it if the trace tells you what you need.

Decisions and Why

Most of the work in v2 was not new structure — it was finding and fixing things that quietly biased v1.

What the Data Actually Looks Like

Implausible bodies

The height field contains values of 12 inches and a stack of exactly 96 inches (a slider hitting its cap); ape index reaches ±30. Under 1% of rows — invisible in a mean, decisive in a quadratic: the 50 most extreme users carried 10.3% of all squared-height leverage. The entire v1 height investigation ran with these in.

Reported heights. Shaded region is kept; values outside are treated as missing.

How much of each climber do we use?

The model reduces every climber-gym pair to a single number: their hardest send. That is 2.76% of all logged sends. It sounds brutal, but 77.5% of sends sit two or more grades below the climber's own max — warm-ups and volume, carrying little information about a ceiling.

Distance of each send below that climber's own maximum at the same gym.

The Gender Problem

Kaya has no gender field, so v1 inferred it from first names with gender_guesser, which returns a label and nothing else. v2 uses nomquamgender, which aggregates 30+ name sources into a calibrated probability with a sample size behind it — 94.6% coverage against 83.6%.

The trap. Misclassification is not random with respect to height. A tall person with a female-leaning name is probably male — P(female) falls from 0.89 at 62in to 0.11 at 74in for the same name. Because gender carries a ~1.6-grade main effect, that leaks straight into the height coefficient and can manufacture a "height helps women" finding out of nothing.

Height is used to sharpen the name prior via Bayes — but deliberately never the climbing data, which would let the model reassign someone's gender to explain their ability and make the two effects mutually reinforcing.

$$P(F \mid \text{name},h) \;=\; \frac{p_{\text{name}}\,\mathcal{N}(h;\mu_F,\sigma_F)} {p_{\text{name}}\,\mathcal{N}(h;\mu_F,\sigma_F) + (1-p_{\text{name}})\,\mathcal{N}(h;\mu_M,\sigma_M)}$$

Does the probability actually mean anything?

Height is an independent check, and it tracks the name probability monotonically across every bin — strong evidence the numbers are calibrated rather than decorative.

The Height Question

Open — three competing explanations, being tested now In progress

Version 1 concluded that height has no effect for male users and a real positive effect for female users. That conclusion now has three possible sources, and they have not yet been separated:

  1. Outlier leverage. A handful of impossible heights dominating a quadratic.
  2. Gender misclassification. Tall female-labelled climbers who are mostly male, and male climbers are stronger on average.
  3. A genuine saturating effect. Reach helps up to a point, then stops — which would look exactly like "no effect for tall men, real effect for shorter women," because 61.5% of men sit above the knee and only 10.9% of women do.

All three predict the same pattern. Distinguishing them is what the current fits are for; results land here as they arrive rather than being guessed at.

One thing already settled: the earlier investigation rejected an asymmetric bump function because its shape looked wrong — left width 1.59in against right width 12.87in. That is not a failed bell curve. That is what a saturating curve looks like when you force a symmetric bump onto it, and no one had fitted that shape directly.

Which shape does the data prefer?

Reading this table

elpd — expected log pointwise predictive density. For each observation the fitted model gives a probability density at the value actually seen; take the log, and sum over all 20,014 of them. Higher means the model put more probability on what really happened. Leave-one-out (LOO) estimates it out of sample: for each row, what the model would have predicted had it not been trained on that row — approximated by reweighting the draws it already has, rather than refitting 20,014 times.

The absolute number is meaningless. It scales with how many observations there are and how coarse the grade scale is. Only differences between models on the same data carry information.

SE of Δ — the standard error of the difference between two models, not of either one alone. Both totals are wildly uncertain (±133 elpd each), but they are uncertain in the same direction: the two models agree on nearly every individual observation and disagree on a few. So the difference is far better determined than either total. It is computed per observation — di = elpdA,i − elpdB,i, then √n × sd(d) — which gives about ±18 here. Treating the models as independent and adding their SEs in quadrature would give ±188, roughly ten times too pessimistic.

A Δ smaller than about two of those SEs is not a ranking, it is noise, and is greyed out above.

Pareto k — the health check on the LOO approximation, which 17% of rows here fail. Rather than refit without each observation, LOO reweights the existing draws by how unlikely that observation was. When one observation dominates the weights, the weight distribution has a heavy tail, and k is the fitted shape of that tail. Above about 0.7 the estimate is unreliable for that observation.

The cause is structural, not a bug. Every climber has their own ability offset, and 59% of them appear exactly once — so removing that one observation removes the only thing informing their parameter, which is precisely the case importance sampling handles worst. Restricting the score to climbers with three or more observations drops the failure rate to 4%, and is what the section below does.

Every form below is fitted on identical data — the same 29-gym network, the same confident-name filter, the same 20,014 observations — so leave-one-out cross-validation can rank them. Only the gaps between models mean anything; the absolute elpd does not.

That table is not measuring what it looks like it is measuring. The next section shows why, and what is left of the ranking afterwards.

Can this comparison tell the models apart at all?

Before believing any ranking, it is worth asking what the measurement's own noise level is. There is a direct way to find out that requires no theory: fit the same model twice. Same model, same data, same settings — only the random seed differs. Whatever separates the two runs is noise, and every gap in the table above has to clear it to mean anything.

Two runs of the identical model scored 31.1 elpd apart. That is larger than the gap between every pair of height forms except the most extreme. Refitting the winning model drops it to fourth place, below shapes it had supposedly beaten. The ranking was reading noise.

Two diagnostics point at the same cause, and it is structural rather than a bug. ploo — cross-validation’s own estimate of how many free parameters the model is effectively using — comes out at 8,400 on 20,014 observations. And an observation here is one (climber, gym) pair, while 59% of climbers contribute exactly one. Hide that observation and nothing at all is left to estimate that climber’s personal ability offset from. The score for those rows is not a prediction; it is a guess.

Which suggests a test that costs nothing. Cross-validation already produces a separate score for every observation, so the totals can be re-added over any subset without refitting anything — the same subset for every model, so the comparison stays fair. If the single-observation climbers are the problem, dropping them should collapse the noise.

Each model’s score relative to the best model in the same column. 0 is best; more negative predicts held-out climbers worse. Compare only down a column — a narrower subset adds up fewer rows, so its totals are smaller for reasons that have nothing to do with the models. The rows labelled “refit” are the same model fitted again; the spread among them is the noise floor, and any gap smaller than it is not a result.

It collapses. Restricted to climbers with three or more observations — still half the data — two runs of the identical model agree to a fraction of a point, and a ranking appears that is many times larger than the noise around it. The share of rows flagged unreliable by the Pareto k diagnostic falls the same way, from 17% to 4%.

Measuring against a fixed yardstick

The table above scores every model against whichever model won that column, and that is a poor yardstick for two reasons. The winner is picked using the same data it is then scored on, so its score is flattered and every other model is measured against an inflated benchmark. And the winner changes from column to column, so the zero point quietly moves and a row cannot be read across the table.

Scoring against a fixed reference fixes both. The natural choice is the model with no height term at all: it is the same model in every column and in both versions, and the distance from it is the question the whole comparison exists to ask — does knowing a climber’s height buy any predictive power?

And a gap needs an error bar, which these never had. The two models being compared score the same rows, and their per-row scores are strongly correlated — both find the same climbers hard to predict. Subtracting row by row before adding up cancels that shared difficulty, and the uncertainty collapses: from about ±145 on either model’s total to ±8–19 on the difference between them. That smaller number is the one that says whether a gap is real.

What survives, stated no more strongly than it should be.

Height matters — at two to three standard errors. Among climbers with three or more sends, adding a height term is worth about +25 ± 9 points in the original version and +17 to +22 ± 8 once the offsets are integrated out. That is a real effect, and it is the column where the two identical refits agree almost exactly (+25.4 and +25.0), which is what makes the column trustworthy. It is not, however, the overwhelming result an unlabelled “25 points” made it sound like.

Which shape height takes is not settled. The saturating curve sits near the bottom in the original version and at the top once the offsets are integrated out. That swing is about 1.5× the error on it, so the two are not in contradiction — they simply do not pin the shape down. Any claim of a specific functional form is reading past what the data supports.

The model used elsewhere on this page is the weakest of the height forms. The curved, gender-split form fails to beat no height term at all in three of the four columns once the offsets are integrated out, and its best column is well inside the error. Both versions agree it belongs near the bottom, so this is not an artefact of the change. The header model should most likely be a straight line.

The all-climbers column remains unusable. Two fits of the identical model score +45.0 and +14.0 there. That 31-point spread is larger than most of the effects in the column. Read the three-or-more column; ignore the first one.

The fix: stop estimating what cannot be estimated

Restricting the score to well-observed climbers is a patch on the symptom. The cause is that the model carries 10,357 ability offsets on 20,014 observations, and 6,156 of them are pinned by a single data point each.

Those offsets cannot simply be deleted — gym corrections are identified precisely by the same climber appearing at two gyms, so the climber term is the whole method. But for a climber with one observation the offset can be integrated out exactly. It enters the ceiling additively and is Gaussian; the observation noise is Gaussian; two Gaussians convolve into one wider Gaussian:

$$-m \sim \mathrm{ExGaussian}\!\left(-c,\ \ \sqrt{\sigma_{\text{link}}^{2} + \sigma_{\text{user}}^{2}},\ \ \nu\right)$$

Same distribution family, one wider spread parameter, nothing approximated and nothing discarded. \(\sigma_{\text{user}}\) is still estimated — from the climbers who have enough observations to say something about it. The parameter count drops from 10,397 to 4,241, and the rows that were unscoreable become scoreable, because their likelihood no longer depends on any parameter unique to them.

What “integrating out” actually means

The phrase deserves unpacking, because it sounds like a simplification and is the opposite of one. Fitting an offset means asking what is this climber’s ability? and committing to a single answer. Integrating it out means declining to answer, and instead asking a question that does not require one: averaged over every ability this climber could plausibly have, weighted by how plausible each is, how likely was what we actually saw? Formally, for a climber with observations \(m_1 \ldots m_k\):

$$p(m_1 \ldots m_k) \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \left[\prod_{j=1}^{k} p\!\left(m_j \mid \epsilon\right)\right]\, \mathcal{N}\!\left(\epsilon;\, 0,\, \sigma_{\text{user}}\right) \, d\epsilon$$

The offset \(\epsilon\) goes in on the right and does not come out on the left. That is the whole trick: the climber’s ability is still fully part of the model, still shaping the answer, but it is no longer a number the fit has to pin down. Nothing is thrown away and no information is lost — the uncertainty that used to be parked in 10,357 separate parameters is instead accounted for honestly in every observation’s likelihood.

For a climber with one observation, the product has one term and the integral has the closed form above — exact, instant, nothing to approximate. For a climber with several, it does not, and has to be computed numerically. That is a one-dimensional integral, however many observations the climber has, because they all share the same single \(\epsilon\). One dimension is cheap; this is the reason the whole approach is affordable.

Where the obvious method fails

The textbook tool for exactly this integral — a smooth function against a Gaussian weight — is Gauss–Hermite quadrature: evaluate the integrand at a handful of cleverly chosen points, take a weighted sum, done. Those points are chosen to suit the Gaussian weight, which here is the prior, spread across every ability a climber might have.

But the thing being integrated is not spread out at all. A climber with 21 logged sends has already told us almost exactly what their ability is; their integrand is a narrow spike roughly \(1/\sqrt{k}\) as wide as the prior, sitting wherever their data puts it. Points placed to cover the prior mostly land where the integrand is indistinguishable from zero, and contribute nothing but runtime.

The fix is adaptive quadrature: before placing any points, find where that climber’s spike actually is and how wide it is, then put the points there. Finding it is a few steps of Newton’s method on the log-integrand, which is cheap because both derivatives are available in closed form. This is standard practice rather than an invention — it is what lme4’s nAGQ option does — but it is the difference between the method being usable and not: 466 ms per evaluation down to 88–134 ms, with a more accurate answer.

One trap worth recording. The ExGaussian log-density is written as three terms that are each infinite when the effort parameter \(\nu\) gets very small, and cancel exactly — in exact arithmetic. In floating point that is inf − inf, which is NaN. NUTS never wandered far enough to hit it, so it sat undetected in the original model for the whole project. Nested sampling starts from the entire prior and hit it immediately, in 2 of 5 random draws. Both implementations now switch to the Normal limit below \(\nu = 0.05\,\sigma\).

Checked three ways before being believed

A quadrature nested inside an ExGaussian, implemented twice, is exactly the kind of code that returns a plausible wrong number in silence. None of these checks would catch a wrong model; all of them catch a wrong implementation, which is the failure actually at risk here.

The third check is the one that nearly went wrong. It first reported a 4,535-unit disagreement, which turned out to be the test’s fault rather than the model’s: asking PyMC for a parameter by name returns the random variable, and evaluating that draws a fresh value from the prior instead of reading the point being tested. A test that fails loudly for its own reasons is a good outcome; the same mistake in a script that produced a number would not have announced itself.

The two versions, side by side

Both models are kept and both are fitted, on identical data with identical settings, differing in exactly one thing. The original is not obsolete: it is the evidence that the change was needed, and a claim that a fix worked is empty without the thing it fixed. The toggle above the chart switches every figure in this section between them.

One comparison that is NOT valid: the two versions’ scores against each other. They are answering different questions. The original predicts an observation given that climber’s fitted ability — and for a climber with one observation, that ability was fitted using the very row being predicted, which flatters it. The marginalized version predicts the observation averaging over an unknown climber’s ability, which is honest and therefore scores lower. A lower number here is the more trustworthy number, not a worse model. Compare scores within a version, never across.

Their parameters, though, are the same parameters. A gym correction means “how much stiffer this gym grades than the network average” in both, measured in grades, on the same 29 gyms. So the question that actually matters can be asked directly, and it is not a question about scores at all: does the answer change, or was this only a repair to the way models were being compared?

The same question for everything else the model estimates. Going in, the parameter to watch was \(\sigma_{\text{user}}\), the spread of natural ability between climbers: with 10,357 free offsets, the original could let per-climber parameters soak up variation that really belongs to the population spread. It did not. It is the least-moved number in the table. The rightmost column gives each shift as a multiple of the uncertainty the original already had on that parameter, which is the only scale on which “did it move” has an answer — below 1 means the shift is smaller than the noise it was already quoted with, and nothing here reaches 0.5.

Verified two ways before being believed. The same likelihood was implemented twice — once in PyTensor for PyMC, once in plain NumPy as a reference — and the two agree to 1.3 × 10−9 relative at the same parameter values. The NumPy version also checks its closed form against brute-force numerical integration of the same integral, agreeing to 10−13. Two independent implementations agreeing is a much stronger statement than one implementation converging.

Four samplers, one posterior

Removing 6,156 parameters did something else besides fixing the cross-validation: it put the model within reach of algorithms that could not touch it before. That is worth using, because a sampler agreeing with itself proves very little.

The concern is concrete. The marginalized likelihood is a numerical integral nested inside a distribution that is itself a sum of two others, and it is written twice — once as a PyTensor graph so PyMC can differentiate it, once in plain NumPy. Code like that does not usually fail loudly. It computes a plausible wrong number. One implementation converging says nothing about whether it converged to the right thing; two implementations and three algorithms landing on the same posterior does.

Why each one, and why none of them on the original model.

PyMC / NUTS uses the gradient of the likelihood to take long, well-aimed steps. It is the only one of the four that can handle thousands of parameters at all, which is why it was the only option before.

emcee runs a population of walkers that propose moves by looking at each other, using no gradients — so it exercises the NumPy implementation, entirely independent of the PyTensor graph. It needs more walkers than parameters and degrades badly past a few tens of dimensions, so on 10,397 parameters it was not merely slow but unusable.

Nested sampling answers a different question: it computes the evidence, which gives Bayes factors directly and touches none of the importance-sampling machinery where every problem above lives. Also unusable above a few tens of parameters.

Bridge sampling estimates the same evidence from draws already in hand, by a different estimator, in minutes. Agreement with nested sampling is evidence for both.

What has held steady across every fit

Different height forms are different models. A number that barely moves as the height block is swapped out underneath it is not an artefact of one specification — and a number that swings is a warning about how much it depended on modelling choices.
