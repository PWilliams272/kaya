The Height Investigation

Height turned out to be the hardest covariate to get right — worth walking through in full, including a mistake caught along the way, because the process is as informative as the result.

Final answer: zero for male users, real polynomial for female users Settled

The bump-function shapes below (kept for the record) didn't hold up to a plain visual sanity check — the gender-split version had left/right widths differing 8× (1.59in vs. 12.87in), which isn't an "optimal height," it's a cliff into a plateau. So the investigation was constrained to three options only: polynomial, linear, or zero — tested with the standard, unconstrained quadratic (γ1·h + γ2·h²) plus a gender interaction, no vertex reparameterization, no bump.

Result: male users show no credible height effect at all — both γ1 and γ2 include zero. Female users show a real quadratic effect — both terms credibly exclude zero (γ1=0.065 [0.033, 0.098], γ2=0.0033 [0.0007, 0.0057]). The parabola's vertex falls at ~58in — right at the bottom edge of the observed height range — so across almost the entire practical range this isn't a peak-in-the-middle bell at all, it's an essentially monotonic, mildly accelerating "taller is better" relationship for female users, with no effect detected for male users.

This closes out the functional-form question — no further iteration planned. What follows is the investigation's history (coarse/fine splines, the vertex-quadratic, the bump function, its null-check, the gender-reconciliation test), kept for the methodological record since the process — including a shape that looked confident right up until a visual gut-check rejected it — is as informative as the final answer.

How we got here (superseded by the final answer above): five functional forms, one at a time

A plain quadratic (the obvious first choice) found essentially no curvature — flat, symmetric uncertainty around zero. That could mean "no real effect," or it could mean a quadratic simply can't represent a bounded optimal-height effect without either being too rigid or exploding at the extremes. Four more targeted tests followed.

f(h) = A · exp(−((h−peak)/width)²), with separate left/right widths so the falloff doesn't have to be symmetric. Amplitude came out at 0.437 [0.230, 0.658] — cleanly excluding zero — with the falloff itself asymmetric: being shorter than the peak costs ability noticeably faster than being taller does.

A methodological catch worth keeping in the record A piecewise-linear spline can only bend exactly at a pre-chosen knot — so its "peak at 71in" partly reflects where the knots were placed, not something the data discovered on its own. This was flagged before it got reported as a clean result, and is the reason the investigation moved to knot-free, structurally-bounded forms instead of trusting the spline's peak location at face value.

Stress-testing the peak: could this be an artifact?

A fair concern: everything above was "hunt for the shape assuming an effect exists." None of it tested whether these methods would report a fake peak on data with no real height effect at all — and the confidence level tracked how tightly each method constrained the shape (bump: confident, splines: borderline, vertex-quadratic: null), which is exactly the kind of pattern that can indicate a prior-driven artifact rather than a real effect.

Synthetic null check Passes

Simulated data through the model's own full generative pipeline — real users' actual heights, gym structure, and gap likelihood — with the true height effect set to exactly zero. Fit the identical bump model to it. Result: fitted amplitude −0.012 [−0.352, 0.479], correctly recovering the null, with peak becoming essentially unidentified — exactly the behavior you'd want when there's nothing for it to lock onto. The bump function does not have a structural bias toward manufacturing a fake peak.

Does the peak differ by gender?

An early spline-based test suggested yes — quite dramatically (66in for male users, 76in for female users) — but that result rested on a 4-parameter interaction term fit against a thin female-tall tail, the same overfitting shape as a rejected ape-index spline elsewhere in this investigation. It needed re-testing with a method less prone to that failure mode.

Gender-shift bump function, reconciliation test Corroborated

Instead of a 4-parameter spline interaction, the bump's peak gets a single gender-scaled shift term — one extra parameter instead of four correlated ones, and much less prone to overfitting a small subgroup. Result: peak_shift = 8.71in [5.98, 11.48], credibly excluding zero. Male-user peak: 64.0in. Female-user peak: 72.7in. Different exact numbers from the spline (66/76in), but the same direction and a similar magnitude, now from an independent, lower-dimensional method.

Worth being honest that this cuts against intuition — a 10-inch-taller optimal height for female users isn't the obvious prior to hold, and gender is inferred from first names via a probabilistic package, not self-reported, which adds its own noise. It's now corroborated by two independent methods rather than one, which is meaningfully more convincing than either alone, but it's not treated as closed.

Held-out predictive comparison Essentially tied

Split users 80/20 (not observations — held-out users get zero personalized signal from either fit, testing whether the height shape itself generalizes). Fit linear-height and bump-height models identically otherwise on the same train split, forward-simulated predicted grades for the 745 held-out users' 1,248 observations, compared to real logged grades.

linear MAE = 1.5011 vs. bump MAE = 1.4997 — bump wins on just 50.2% of observations, mean paired difference 0.0014 ± 0.0015 (smaller than 1 standard error). No detectable predictive improvement.

This doesn't contradict the credible-interval evidence above — it answers a different question. For a brand-new climber, most predictive error comes from not knowing their personal ability offset at all (σ ≈ 1.6 grade units, freshly drawn), which swamps a height effect of magnitude ≈ 0.4–0.9.

Why this history is superseded Everything above (peak locations, the bump function, its null check, the gender-shift reconciliation, the held-out MAE numbers) was built on the bump function's asymmetric-Gaussian shape — which turned out, on a plain visual check, to look nothing like a believable "optimal height" curve. The final answer section at the top of this page supersedes all of it: zero effect for male users, a real quadratic (practically monotonic in range) effect for female users. One honest gap: the held-out predictive comparison above was run against the bump model, not this final quadratic — re-running it wasn't worth the added time given how small the quadratic effect is, but that means the "does this actually improve prediction" question is technically still open for the final model specifically.
