A second sampler, and the one number it will not agree on

Integrating the climber offsets out did something beyond fixing the cross-validation: it took the model from 10,397 parameters to 40, which put it within reach of algorithms that could not touch it before. That is worth spending a night of compute on, because a sampler agreeing with itself proves very little.

The concern is specific. The marginalized likelihood is a numerical integral nested inside a distribution that is itself a sum of two others, and it exists twice — once as a PyTensor graph so PyMC can differentiate it, once in plain NumPy. Code shaped like that does not usually fail loudly; it computes a plausible wrong number. One implementation converging says nothing about whether it converged to the right thing.

emcee is the check. It is an ensemble sampler: 128 walkers explore at once, each proposing its next move by looking at where the other walkers currently are. It uses no gradient at all, and it runs the NumPy implementation — so it shares neither the algorithm nor the code with PyMC/NUTS. It also needs more walkers than parameters and degrades badly past a few tens of dimensions, which is why it was not merely slow on the original model but unusable.

One run: runs/results/emcee_lin2.npz, the linear height form on the same 29-gym confident-names network as v3_lin_marg, the PyMC fit it is compared against throughout this section.

How to read an ensemble trace

Eight of the 128 walkers, thinned to 400 steps. Same reading as any trace plot: you want boring — a dense even band with no trend and no flat stretches, with every line living in the same horizontal region.

The one thing that reads differently here is width. Walkers in an ensemble sampler are not supposed to be independent, so lines moving together is normal rather than alarming. What matters is that the band stays put.

Has it settled?

How to read the running mean

The running mean of every draw up to that step. The shaded band is the spread across the walkers’ own running means — where 90% of the 128 individually sit.

Flat line, closing band is what a settled chain looks like: the estimate has stopped moving and the walkers are converging on it. A line still trending at the right-hand edge means the warm-up was too short and the answer is still being found.

The dotted line is PyMC’s posterior mean for the same parameter, so the two samplers can be compared at a glance rather than through the table.

Why 1.9 million draws are worth 3,500

Autocorrelation, and what τ is

How correlated a draw is with the draw n steps earlier. It starts at 1 by definition and decays; the faster it falls to zero, the more each new step is telling you.

τ, the integrated autocorrelation time, is the area under that curve — roughly, how many steps the ensemble takes to produce one draw’s worth of genuinely new information. Lower is better. Effective sample size is just the raw draw count divided by it, which is why 1.92 million draws at τ = 542 come to about 3,500.

A slow, smooth decay means correlated, not stuck. Stuck looks different: a curve that stays near 1 and never comes down.

Is any single walker misbehaving?

Every walker, on two measures. Left: the fraction of proposals each accepted — 0.2–0.4 is the healthy band, too low means it is stuck and too high means it is barely moving. Right: each walker’s own posterior mean for the selected parameter. A flat scatter is what agreement looks like; one point sitting clear of the rest is a walker that found somewhere the others did not, and would invalidate the pooled mean.

Where a third and fourth sampler would go

Two samplers agreeing is much stronger than one converging, but both of these are still Markov chain methods, and they share the failure mode above: a posterior geometry that traps one can trap the other. Two more are queued, and they are queued because they fail differently.

Nested sampling answers a different question entirely — it computes the evidence, which gives Bayes factors between height forms directly and touches none of the importance-sampling machinery where the cross-validation problems on this page live. Like emcee, it is unusable above a few tens of parameters, so it too only became possible once the climber offsets were integrated out.

Bridge sampling estimates that same evidence from draws already in hand, by a completely different estimator, in minutes rather than hours. Agreement between the two is evidence for both; disagreement localises the problem to the estimator rather than the model.
