Climbers improve fastest at the bottom of the scale and barely at all near the top

This matters to the gym result, not just as a curiosity. The model treats a climber as a fixed quantity — there is no \(t\) anywhere in \(C_{u,g}\). So if someone logged at one gym in 2022 and another in 2025, three years of getting better is charged to the second gym. The model has no other place to put it.

How this is measured

Each climber’s sends are bucketed into 90-day windows. A window with at least 5 sends gets a level: the hardest grade sent in it. Windowed, not cumulative — a running max-so-far only ever goes up, which would manufacture improvement out of noise. A windowed max can fall.

Consecutive windows up to 15 months apart then give a rate in grades per year.

Grades gained per year, by current grade — higher is faster improvement. Dashed is the naive estimator, dotted the one-year window shown for contrast, solid the three-month window the model uses. The vertical scale is pinned to keep the useful curves readable, so the naive one leaves the frame in both directions; that it does not fit is the point, and its values are in the table above. The shape survives every correction — what the corrections change is the magnitude.

The rate depends on how long you watch, which is why it is fitted across every window at once

Nothing about the measurement says how long the watching period \(\Delta t\) should be, and that turns out to matter more than any of the arithmetic. Hold everything else fixed and change only the window:

At V1 the answer moves from +0.88 grades a year to +0.22 depending on nothing but how long you look. The high bins barely move at all. A number that swings by a factor of four on an arbitrary choice is not a measurement of anything.

A rate has to compound, and at the bottom of the scale this one does not. Saying V1 climbers improve at 0.88 grades a year is a prediction: watch for a year and they should gain 0.88 grades. They gain 0.22, which is 4.8 standard errors away. The V1 group gains about a fifth of a grade after three months — and still about a fifth of a grade after twelve. That is a one-time step, not a rate, and dividing a step by the window you happened to pick just reports your choice back to you.

So the estimator stops picking a window and uses them all. A genuine rate means the gain grows in proportion to elapsed time, so the fit is a line through the origin across all six horizons at once, weighted by \(w_h = 1/\mathrm{SE}_h^2\), and the fit reports whether that proportionality actually holds:

$$\overline{\Delta \ell}(h) = f(v)\cdot h \ ,\qquad \hat f(v) = \frac{\sum_h w_h\, h\, \overline{\Delta \ell}(h)} {\sum_h w_h\, h^2}$$

This is the one estimator here that can be wrong in a way you can see. If a bin were really a step rather than a rate, its points would not lie on a line through the origin and the \(\chi^2\) would say so. It comes out near one in almost every bin.

The bin labels are lying, and always toward the middle. The starting level \(\ell_{w_0}\) is itself a noisy max, so each bin is stocked with climbers whose real level sits closer to the population average. Measured at the start of the window, the “V1” bin averages V2.0 and the “V9” bin averages V8.6. The horizontal axis is compressed by about a grade at each end, so every slope here understates the real grade dependence — the safe direction to err when the claim is that advancement slows down.
The obvious estimator is broken by regression to the mean, and says V11 climbers lose grades Settled — discarded

Binning the change \(\ell_2-\ell_1\) by the starting level \(\ell_1\) asks one measurement to do two jobs: decide which bin the climber goes in, and serve as the baseline that gets subtracted. A lucky window inflates \(\ell_1\), which files the climber a grade too high and makes the measured change come out a grade too low. The estimator tilts the curve by construction.

The tell is that it returns a physically impossible number at one end: the naive estimator has V11 climbers losing 3.5 grades a year. Nobody does that. The fix is to hand the binning job to a third window \(w_0\) that takes no part in the subtraction, so the luck that landed someone in the V8 bin cannot tilt their measured change.

The same trap outside climbing. Suppose you want to know whether shorter children grow faster, and your tape measure is sloppy. If one measurement both decides who counts as short and serves as the baseline you subtract, every child accidentally measured too short is filed as short and appears to grow extra. You will “discover” that short children grow faster — with a clean, monotone curve — even if every child grows at exactly the same rate. Measure on Monday to decide who is short, measure growth from Tuesday to Friday, and Monday’s slop cannot manufacture Tuesday-to-Friday growth.
Date and gym correction really do correlate — the mechanism is not settled Open

Across the network, a gym’s mean observation date and its fitted correction correlate at r = 0.61, a slope of 0.83 grades per year of mean date. Within a single brand it is r = 0.55, so it is not just an artefact of brands entering the data at different times.

Measured climber advancement is far too small to explain that on its own — at +0.14 grades/year around the middle of the scale against an observed 0.83. So the correlation is real and the mechanism is not established: advancement is one contributor, not the explanation. This is the largest known open question in the model, and the reason advancement enters as a fixed interpolated offset rather than as a fitted time term.
