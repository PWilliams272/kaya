Seven shapes for height, and a leaderboard that cannot be read

Does being tall help on a boulder problem? The honest answer is that it depends what shape you let the answer take. A straight line says taller is always better. A curve allows an optimum. A saturating form says height helps until it stops helping. Each is a different claim, and the only way to choose between them is to fit all of them on the same data and compare how well each predicts a send it has not seen.

So that is what this is: seven height forms, refit together, on one network of 29 gyms, with a long warm-up so that no form is handicapped by a sampler that had not settled. Roughly a day of compute.

Read this before reading the table

Seven models each with a score invites exactly one behaviour: sort the column and announce a winner. Everything about the presentation of a leaderboard encourages it, which is why the warning belongs above the table rather than beneath it.

The sweep

R-hat compares the variance between chains to the variance within them; 1.00 is perfect agreement and lower is better. Effective draws is how many genuinely independent samples the chains produced, after discounting for the fact that consecutive draws are correlated; higher is better. Both are computed across the nine parameters this page actually quotes, not across the 4,201 per-climber offsets nobody reads.

Chains or draws — the measurement that changed the plan

This sweep was deliberately run as four short chains rather than four long ones, on the reasoning that chains can be added later if the diagnostics demand it. That reasoning had never been checked. Checking it required no new compute at all — the finished traces can be subsetted, scored at one, two, three and four chains, and again at a quarter, half, three quarters and all of their length.

Measured on v7_v3_quad, the quadratic form, by subsetting its own chains and draws — so this is a property of this posterior on this data, not a general claim about samplers.
