What was measured and did not survive

These are results, not caveats. Each one was measured, each one changed what the model does or how its numbers may be read, and each is here with the number that settled it. The full working record is on Archive — v2 Notes.

Two runs of the identical model scored 31.1 elpd apart, so most of the model comparison could not hear itself Settled — fixed

Refitting the same model on the same data and scoring it twice gave answers 31.1 elpd apart. That run-to-run noise was larger than most of the effects being compared, which means the original height-form ranking was largely measuring the sampler. Integrating out the per-climber offsets brought it to 2.9 on the all-climbers column — the column that was unusable is now usable.

On well-observed climbers only, the two versions sit at 0.5 and 3.3 and cannot be separated: there are only two converged refits per version, far too few to rank two small numbers. Restricting the score and integrating out the offsets turn out to fix the same problem; doing both is not measurably better than doing either.

The all-climbers column is unusable and should not be read Settled — parked

Two fits of the identical model score +45.0 and +14.0 there. A 31-point spread between identical fits is larger than most of the effects in the column. The cause is structural: the model carried 10,397 ability offsets, and a majority of climbers appear at only one gym, so those offsets are pinned by a single data point each. Integrating out the singletons drops that to 4,241. Read the three-or-more-sends column; ignore the first one.

The reliability signal buys nothing — ρ fits at essentially zero Settled — null

\(\rho\) scales how much a climber’s logging reliability changes the rate at which their logged max approaches their true ceiling. It fits at essentially zero, so moving it does nothing to the predicted gap. The term is kept because its being zero is informative — how completely someone logs does not, on this data, tell you how close their logged max sits to their limit.

One refit in three did not converge at all, so any single fit needs its diagnostics checked before it is quoted Open — live risk

One refit reached R-hat 1.44 with an effective sample size of 8. It is excluded from the noise band above, because a broken chain is not a measure of sampling noise — but the failure itself is the finding: this model does not fit dependably on every seed. R-hat compares variation between chains to variation within one; 1.00 is perfect agreement and anything above about 1.01 means the chains did not explore the same distribution.

This is why the payload builders now gate on convergence rather than merely reporting it — see src/kaya/convergence.py. A fit above R-hat 1.2 cannot become the reference other models are scored against, and scripts/build_v2_results.py refuses to publish at all if the primary fit fails.

Body measurements had to be filtered before any of this — under 1% of rows carried 10.3% of the leverage Settled

The height field contains values of 12 inches and a stack of exactly 96 inches (a slider hitting its cap); ape index reaches ±30. Under 1% of rows — invisible in a mean, decisive in a quadratic: the 50 most extreme users carried 10.3% of all squared-height leverage. The entire v1 height investigation ran with these in. Values outside 4′0″–7′0″ and |ape index| > 12 in are now treated as missing, not believed.

Inferring gender from names leaks into the height coefficient unless height is used to sharpen it Settled

Kaya has no gender field. Misclassification is not random with respect to height: a tall person with a female-leaning name is probably male — P(female) falls from 0.89 at 62 in to 0.11 at 74 in for the same name. Because gender carries a ~1.6-grade main effect, that leaks straight into the height coefficient and can manufacture a “height helps women” finding out of nothing.

v2 uses nomquamgender, which aggregates 30+ name sources into a calibrated probability with a sample size behind it — 94.6% coverage against v1’s 83.6% — and sharpens it with height via Bayes. Deliberately never with the climbing data, which would let the model reassign someone’s gender to explain their ability and make the two effects mutually reinforcing.

One comparison that is NOT valid: the two versions’ scores against each other. They answer different questions. The original predicts an observation given that climber’s fitted ability — and for a climber with one observation, that ability was fitted using the very row being predicted, which flatters it. The marginalized version predicts averaging over an unknown climber’s ability, which is honest and therefore scores lower. A lower number here is the more trustworthy number, not a worse model. Compare scores within a version, never across.
