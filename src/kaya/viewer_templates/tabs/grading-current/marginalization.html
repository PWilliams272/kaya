Integrating the climber out

The previous section leaves a specific problem. The model gives every climber a personal ability offset, and for 59% of them that offset is fitted from a single observation — the very observation the cross-validation then tries to hold out. There are two ways out, and they are quite different in character.

  • Score only the rows the method can see. Add up the held-out scores over climbers with three or more observations instead of all of them. Cheap, honest, and it throws away most of the data.
  • Remove the offset from the model. Not by deleting it — that would assert every climber is identical — but by integrating over it: considering every ability the climber might have, weighted by how plausible each one is, and adding up the results. This is marginalization.

What marginalizing actually does

For a climber with one send, the probability of what they did is no longer evaluated at one guessed ability. It is averaged across all of them:

$$p(y) \;=\; \int p(y \mid a)\; p(a)\; \mathrm{d}a$$

The critical thing about this is that it is not an approximation and not a different model. That integral is the likelihood of the same model the previous sections describe, written down without the offset in it. Sampling \(a\) and then ignoring it gives the identical answer, given infinitely many draws. Doing the integral exactly just removes the “given infinitely many draws” — and with it the Monte Carlo noise those draws contribute.

The distribution being integrated over is not the population’s. It is this climber’s. A climber’s ceiling is the population intercept, plus their height, gender, ape-index and visit terms, plus the gym correction, plus a personal offset. Only the last term is integrated out — everything to its left stays, so the bell curve being swept is centred where that climber’s profile puts it.

This is why the height and gender effects survive marginalization rather than being destroyed by it. Taller climbers’ sends are scored against a curve centred differently from shorter climbers’, and the fitted shift between those centres is the height effect. \(\sigma_{\text{user}}\) — 1.63 grades — describes only what is left over once the profile is accounted for.

Why the product goes inside the integral

For a climber with several sends the arithmetic is not the same thing done repeatedly. Their sends are correlated, because they share one ability: a strong climber’s sends are all high together. So the integral has to run over the joint probability of everything they did at once.

$$p(y_1,\dots,y_n) \;=\; \int \Big[\prod_{r=1}^{n} p(y_r \mid a)\Big]\, p(a)\, \mathrm{d}a$$

Moving the product outside — \(\prod_r \int p(y_r \mid a) p(a)\,\mathrm{d}a\) — would assert that the climber re-rolls their ability before every session, treating four sends by one person as four sends by four strangers. That destroys exactly the information that makes well-observed climbers valuable, and it inflates the effective sample size, so every uncertainty on the page would come back too small.

Why only the single-send climbers

The fitted model integrates out the offsets of climbers with one observation and leaves everyone else’s in place, taking it from 10,397 parameters to 4,241. That boundary is an engineering compromise, not a statistical principle, and it is worth saying so plainly.

PyMC’s sampler needs gradients, and the quadrature points are placed by solving for each climber’s most likely ability — so the point locations depend on the parameters being differentiated with respect to. Closed form has no such problem, which is why the free case is the one that made it into the fitted model.

The marginalize-everyone version exists too. A second, gradient-free implementation integrates out all 10,357 climbers and comes to 40 parameters. It is what the independent samplers — emcee, nested sampling — run on, since they never needed gradients and so never hit the problem above. It is a cross-check on the fitted model, not a replacement for it.

Did it work?

The same diagnostic as the previous section, on both versions, over the same rows and the same number of draws. Single-send climbers are the target. The other rows are the control — marginalizing single-send climbers must leave them alone, and if they had moved, something other than the intended change would have happened.

Single-send rows go from 38.0% unusable to 0.0%, and their largest importance weight from 133× a typical one to 1.1× — an essentially even spread, which is what healthy importance sampling looks like. Median Pareto k falls from 0.62 to −0.07. The control rows shift by a percentage point at most — the two versions are separate sampling runs, so rows the change does not touch still differ by the ordinary run-to-run noise, and that is what a control looks like when it works.

This is less a repair than a disappearance. In the marginalized model a single-send climber has no offset to remove, so holding their row out no longer widens anything and there is nothing left for the weights to correct. The diagnostic is not “better”; the failure mode it detects is absent.

What it costs. Two things, both worth stating.

Per-climber abilities are no longer sampled for the single-send climbers. In practice that costs nothing — their “estimate” was essentially the prior anyway — and the distribution can be reconstructed afterwards in closed form if it is ever wanted.

Held-out scores are not comparable across the two versions. For a single-send climber the question has genuinely changed: the original model asks “predict this row using an ability fitted from this row,” and the marginalized model asks “predict this row for a climber whose ability is unknown.” The second is harder and scores lower. That is the honest question scoring worse than the flattering one, not a worse model. Compare within a version, never across.
