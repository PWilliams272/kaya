One number per gym is not enough

Everything above gives each gym a single grading correction: one number saying how much harder or softer it grades than the network average. That number carries two assumptions, and neither has been checked. It assumes the gym's offset is the same for a V2 climber and a V9 climber. And it assumes the offset is the same in 2021 and in 2026.

Both are testable without fitting anything, and both fail. The test is the same identity the whole model rests on. For a climber who logs at two gyms, subtract their two levels and the climber disappears entirely — ability, height, gender, and any improvement all cancel, because it is the same person on both sides:

$$\text{level}_A - \text{level}_B \;=\; \text{correction}_A - \text{correction}_B \;+\; \text{noise}$$

What is left is a statement about the two gyms and nothing else. Regress it against the climber's ability and you are asking whether a gym grades differently at the top of its range than the bottom — grade compression. Regress the same difference against the calendar date and you are asking whether the gym's standard moved — gym drift. Same trick, perpendicular axes.

Why a hundred slopes is not yet an answer

Run that regression for one pair of gyms and you get one number. Run it for every pair with enough shared climbers and you get about a hundred numbers, and they scatter. The temptation is to report the spread of those numbers as the effect. That would be wrong, and badly so: two things produce the scatter, and only one of them is the finding.

The first is real differences between gyms. The second is measurement noise — each of those hundred numbers came from limited data and has its own error bar, and a hundred noisy estimates of one identical quantity would still scatter. Here the median error bar is nearly as large as the whole observed spread, so most of what the eye reads as “gyms differ” is noise.

Separating them is possible because every regression reports its own error bar. The noise does not have to be guessed. The procedure, used identically for both effects below:

  • Q measures each estimate's distance from the average in units of its own error bar, squares it, and adds up. If a deviation is pure noise, that squared distance averages exactly 1 — so summing over \(k\) pairs should land near \(k-1\). Dividing by the error bar is what makes it fair: a wild estimate with a huge error bar contributes almost nothing.
  • df is that \(k-1\). It is where Q lands when nothing real is happening — a prediction, not a pass mark. The comparison is Q against df.
  • I² = (Q − df) / Q is the excess as a share of the total: what fraction of the scatter is real. Higher means more signal.
  • τ converts that excess back into interpretable units. Q lives in “squared error bars”; τ is grades, or grades per year.

Grade compression: gyms tilt across their own range

Read top to bottom: the boring explanation predicts Q near df, would rarely push it past the critical value, and Q came in far above both. τ is the number to quote.

A straight line is not enough either

If a gym's correction tilts with ability, the next question is whether it tilts in a straight line. Gym setters plausibly compress the top of their range specifically — the hard problems bunch together because there are few of them and nobody wants to grade the outlier — which is a curve, not a tilt. Same design, one term further.

Gym drift: the standard moves

Nothing forces “V6” to mean the same thing in 2026 as it did in 2021. Setters change, management changes, a gym decides its V6s have been too generous. The obstacle to measuring it is that three unrelated things push grades at a gym upward over time: climbers improve, the gym drifts, and a different population shows up. Plotting average grade per year cannot tell them apart.

The paired difference separates them. Compare one climber, in one 90-day window, at two gyms. Same person, so ability cancels. Same moment, so improvement cancels. Present at both, so selection cancels. Then watch that difference across calendar years.

Does the 90-day window let improvement leak back in?

“Same moment” is an approximation — 90 days is not zero, and beginners gain fastest of all, around 0.27 grades a year. If a climber visits one gym early in a window and the other late, part of the difference is their own gain. Two checks: control for the within-window date gap directly, and halve the window, which mechanically halves the gap.

Controlling the gap moves τ by under 1%. Halving the window moves it up, not down — the decisive direction, since contamination by improvement would have shrunk under a tighter window. The rise is most likely a cleaner measurement rather than a stronger effect.

Two routes to the same number

τ describes pairs of gyms, not gyms. Getting to each gym's own rate can be done two entirely different ways, which makes their agreement a genuine check on the arithmetic.

Does drift explain the timing confound?

A gym's correction correlates with when its climbers logged, strongly enough to be worth explaining. Climber improvement accounts for only about 8% of it. Drift was the other obvious suspect — and the right test is not whether fast-drifting gyms have odd corrections, but whether removing each gym's accumulated drift shrinks the relationship.

What this means for the model

Three changes follow, none of them yet fitted. The correction should be a curve in climber ability rather than a constant, and it must be a function of the climber's latent ability rather than the grade they logged — using the observed grade to select its own correction is circular. The correction should carry a per-gym drift rate in calendar time. And both need a zero-sum constraint, because only relative compression and relative drift are identifiable: a shift shared by every gym is absorbed into the ability scale, or cancels in the differences, and no amount of data recovers it.

The drift term has a prerequisite the others do not. The model's dataset is one row per climber per gym and carries no dates at all, so fitting drift means rebuilding the dataset in time-resolved form. That is the strongest argument for doing so — not to fit how fast climbers improve, which is already measured from better data than the model has.
