Kaya records only sends, so the standard model is not identified — everything else follows from working around that

This is an item-response-theory (IRT) model. IRT is the family of models behind standardised testing: it estimates a test-taker’s latent ability and a question’s difficulty at the same time, from nothing but who got what right. Here climbs are the questions, climbers are the test-takers, and a send is a correct answer.

The wrinkle is that Kaya logs only sends — never failed attempts. A standard IRT likelihood needs both, so it is not identified on this data. Every structural choice below exists to work around that one missing piece.

Ability sits on the grade scale, so a correction reads directly in grades

Each climber \(u\) has a latent ability, where \(\beta_0\) is baseline ability (the grade an average climber sends at an average gym), \(\mathbf{x}_u\) is that climber’s covariate row, and \(\sigma_{\text{user}}\) is the spread of natural ability between climbers:

$$\text{ability}_u = \beta_0 + \mathbf{x}_u^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \sigma_{\text{user}}\,\tilde\epsilon_u, \qquad \tilde\epsilon_u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

\(\mathbf{x}_u\) holds gender, height terms, ape-index terms and two missingness indicators, each column centred to mean zero, which makes \(\beta_0\) orthogonal to every slope. Ability, grades and gym corrections all live on the same scale — V-grades — which is what makes a correction of “+0.4” readable as “four tenths of a grade stiffer.”

Gym corrections are forced to sum to zero, because otherwise the model can slide

Ability and correction are identified only up to an additive shift — add a grade to every climber and subtract one from every gym and the data look identical. Constraining the corrections to sum to zero exactly pins it down, and makes “relative to the average gym” true by construction rather than by convention. \(\sigma_{\text{gym}}\) is the spread of grading style across gyms:

$$\text{gym}_g = \sigma_{\text{gym}}\,\tilde\delta_g, \qquad \tilde\delta \sim \text{ZeroSumNormal}(0,1)$$

A climber’s ceiling at a gym, \(C_{u,g}\), is then just the two added:

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g$$

Nobody sends their limit every session, so the logged max is a biased view of the ceiling

The hardest grade someone logged at a gym sits at or below their true ceiling by a non-negative gap. The gap’s rate \(\lambda_{u,g}\) rises with how many chances they have had to find their limit — \(\tilde n_{u,g}\) is their visit count and \(\tilde r_u\) their reliability signal, both standardised:

$$\log \lambda_{u,g} = \log\lambda_0 + \kappa\,\tilde n_{u,g} + \rho\,\tilde r_u$$
$$\text{gap}_{u,g}\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda_{u,g}), \qquad m_{u,g} = C_{u,g} - \text{gap}_{u,g} + \varepsilon,\ \ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{link}})$$

A Normal minus an Exponential is an Exponentially Modified Gaussian (ExGaussian), so the gap integrates out in closed form and never has to be sampled:

$$-m_{u,g} \;\sim\; \text{ExGaussian}\!\left(-C_{u,g},\ \sigma_{\text{link}},\ 1/\lambda_{u,g}\right)$$

That removes one latent variable per observation — 32,501 of them on this network — and turns the likelihood into an ordinary observed node, so LOO (leave-one-out cross-validation) and posterior predictive checks work natively. The closed form was verified against numerical integration of the original formulation to 1×10−8.

Height enters as one curve per gender, blended by probability rather than by label

Height and ape index (wingspan minus height) are near-independent here (\(r = +0.137\)), so their shapes are specified separately. \(\tilde h\) and \(\tilde a\) are centred at the median and scaled to standard deviations. \(G\) is the probability the climber is female, so \(1-G\) and \(G\) are the weights:

$$f_{\text{height}}(\tilde h,\ G) \;=\; \underbrace{(1-G)\left(\gamma_1^{M}\tilde h + \gamma_2^{M}\tilde h^{2}\right)}_{\text{male users}} \;+\; \underbrace{G\left(\gamma_1^{F}\tilde h + \gamma_2^{F}\tilde h^{2}\right)}_{\text{female users}}$$

\(G\) is a probability, not a hard label. A climber whose name is 70% female contributes 0.7 of a row to the female curve and 0.3 to the male one, so uncertainty about the name propagates into the height estimate instead of being silently rounded away.

What is actually fitted. The sampler does not see \(\gamma^{M}\) and \(\gamma^{F}\) as two free pairs. It fits a baseline and a difference — \(\gamma^{M}_{k}=\gamma_k\) and \(\gamma^{F}_{k}=\gamma_k+\gamma_k^{\times}\) — which is the same function, but lets the prior sit on the thing under test. A tight \(\gamma_k^{\times}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,\text{small})\) shrinks the two curves toward each other, so a gender difference has to be earned from the data rather than appearing by default.

Ape index enters on its own, with the same quadratic shape but no gender split — \(\delta_1\) and \(\delta_2\) are its linear and curvature coefficients, in grades per standard deviation of ape index:

$$f_{\text{ape}}(\tilde a) \;=\; \delta_1\tilde a + \delta_2\tilde a^{2}$$

Six height shapes are fitted, because they encode different claims about the world

The gender-blended quadratic above is one candidate of six. The others are a plain quadratic, a straight line, zero (no height term at all), a monotone saturating form — “reach helps until you have enough of it, then stops paying” — and a vertex quadratic that estimates the best height \(p\) directly rather than implying it through a ratio. \(A\) is the total height a saturating curve can buy in grades, \(h_0\) the height at which it is halfway there, \(s\) how sharply it turns over, and \(\kappa_h\) the curvature of the vertex form:

$$f_{\text{sat}}(\tilde h) \;=\; A\,\operatorname{logistic}\!\left(\frac{\tilde h - h_0}{s}\right) \qquad f_{\text{vtx}}(\tilde h) \;=\; -\kappa_h\left(\tilde h - p\right)^{2}$$

All six are compared by LOO (leave-one-out cross-validation) on identical data. What that comparison concluded is its own section; this one is about what each form is capable of saying.

Play with the parameters — each form has things it cannot say

One card per form: the equation, the shape it produces, and a slider for every parameter in it. The point is to see what each form can and cannot say — no setting of the linear sliders will produce a peak, and no setting of the saturating sliders will produce a decline. Where a fit has landed, the sliders start at the fitted values; the rest start at illustrative ones and are labelled as such.

Vertical axis is the height contribution to ability, in V-grades relative to the median climber — higher is better. Horizontal axis is height in inches. In every card the left band is where female users sit (64.2 ± 2.8 in) and the right band is male users (69.2 ± 3.3 in), each one standard deviation wide. All six cards share a vertical scale so their shapes are directly comparable; drag a slider to an extreme and the axis grows rather than clipping the curve.

Does centring height at the median force the peak to sit there? No — worth being explicit about, because it looks like it should. Centring only fixes where \(\tilde h=0\) falls; it says nothing about where the curve turns over. For \(f(\tilde h)=\gamma_1\tilde h+\gamma_2\tilde h^2\) the vertex sits at \(\tilde h^{*}=-\gamma_1/2\gamma_2\), which the data are free to put anywhere — v1’s own fitted coefficients implied a peak roughly 10 inches below the median.

What centring does change is the implied prior on that peak. A ratio of two normals is Cauchy-like — heavy-tailed and piled up near zero — so the plain quadratic quietly favours a peak near the median, and gives a badly-behaved interval whenever \(\gamma_2\) is close to zero. The vertex quadratic makes the peak \(p\) a parameter with a prior of its own, so it reports a credible interval directly instead of propagating error through a division. Both forms are fitted; if they disagree about where the best height is, that disagreement is itself the finding.
