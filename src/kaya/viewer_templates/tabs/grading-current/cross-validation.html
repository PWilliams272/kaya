What the held-out score can and cannot see

Every model comparison above rests on one number: how well a model predicts observations it was not fitted on. That number is doing a lot of work, so it is worth opening up — because the way it is computed is not the obvious way, and for most of the climbers in this data it does not mean what it appears to mean.

Nobody refits twenty thousand times

The obvious approach is leave-one-out: hide a row, refit, predict the hidden row, repeat. That is 20,014 refits at about 85 minutes each — roughly three years of computing for a single model, and there are seven of them. So the model is fitted once, and what would have happened without each row is estimated from that one fit by importance sampling.

Importance sampling in one sentence. If you want the average height in Norway but your sample is Swedish, you are not stuck — provided you know how the two populations differ, you count each Swede more or less heavily to match Norway. Samples drawn from the wrong distribution, re-weighted to answer a question about the right one.

Here the “wrong” distribution is the posterior fitted on all the data, and the right one is the posterior that would have been fitted without row i. Bayes’ rule connects them: removing an observation means dividing out its likelihood. So the weight on posterior draw \(s\) is just

$$w_s \;=\; \frac{1}{p\!\left(y_i \mid \theta_s\right)}$$

One over how well that draw explained the row being held out. The logic is neat: a draw that fits row \(i\) unusually well is over-represented precisely because row \(i\) was in the fit, so to pretend it was absent you push that draw back down.

The objection that makes this click

“But a one-row climber’s ability is fitted almost perfectly to that row. So the likelihood is large, and the weight is tiny. Where is the problem?”

Correct, and beside the point. Importance weights are normalised — divided by their own total — so their absolute size never matters. Only their spread does. A uniformly tiny set of weights is perfectly healthy. What destroys the estimate is a handful of draws hundreds of times larger than the rest, because then the answer rests on those few draws instead of on all 2,000.

And that is exactly what one observation produces. With the row in the fit, that climber’s ability is pinned to a spread of about half a grade. Take the row away and nothing is left to pin it, so it falls back to the prior — a spread of 1.63 grades, more than three times wider. The distribution being aimed at is far wider than the one that was sampled, so its bulk sits where the draws almost never went, and the few that strayed out there have to carry all of it.

Pareto k is where this diagnosis becomes a number. The standard repair for heavy-tailed weights is to fit a generalised Pareto distribution to the largest ~20% of them and cap the wild ones; \(k\) is that distribution’s shape parameter, so it falls out of the repair for free, one value per row. Below 0.5 the weights have finite variance and everything behaves. Above 0.7 the estimate converges too slowly to trust at any realistic number of draws. Lower is better. The threshold is an empirical convention from simulation rather than a theorem — more draws buy tolerance for a slightly heavier tail.

So the headline score is partly fiction. On the original model 17.1% of all rows come back above the 0.7 threshold, and among climbers with a single observation — 59% of climbers — it is 38.0%. Those rows are not measurements. Two things can be done about it, and both were: score only the rows the method can actually see, and change the model so the problem stops existing.
