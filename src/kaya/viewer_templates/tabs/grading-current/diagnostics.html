The sampler hits its tree-depth cap on essentially every iteration, and that is structural

Reported per parameter block, because some of this model mixes well and some does not — and the difference decides which numbers on this page can be trusted.

Plain-language guide to each diagnostic and what you want it to say. The numbers for the selected fit are below.

The sampler reaches maximum tree depth (1,023 leapfrog steps per draw) on essentially every iteration. The cause is structural rather than something reparameterisation fixes: 59% of climbers appear at only one gym, so for those climbers the personal ability offset, the observation noise and the gap all compete to explain a single residual. Four separate reparameterisations were tried; none moved it.

Zero divergences with tree depth at the cap is a specific diagnosis, not a clean bill of health. Divergences say the sampler fell off the surface; hitting the depth cap says it never did, but had to inch along to stay on. Together they describe a posterior that is extremely elongated rather than sharply curved — hard, not broken. The cost is wall-clock time and effective sample size, not correctness.

What R̂ = 1.02 actually costs you

R̂ (R-hat) is quoted as a pass/fail all over this page, which invites two wrong readings: that 1.00 is a floor, and that 1.02 is a rounding error. Both are wrong, and the chains on disk can settle it rather than being asked to be taken on trust.

Four chains are run from different random starting points. If they have all converged on the same answer, how far apart the chains sit should match how much each one wanders on its own. That is the whole idea, and the statistic is the ratio of those two things. For \(m\) chains of \(n\) draws, with \(\bar\theta_j\) the mean of chain \(j\):

$$B = \frac{n}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(\bar\theta_j - \bar\theta\right)^2 \qquad W = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} s_j^2$$ $$\widehat{\operatorname{var}}^{+} = \frac{n-1}{n}W + \frac{1}{n}B \qquad \hat R = \sqrt{\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{var}}^{+}}{W}}$$

\(B\) is the between-chain variance, scaled by \(n\) so it is comparable to a variance of individual draws. \(W\) is the within-chain variance. Under perfect mixing the two are equal and \(\hat R = 1\). Lower is better.

Rearranged, it stops hiding what it means.
$$\frac{B}{W} \;=\; n\left(\hat R^{2} - 1\right) + 1$$
That ratio is roughly the autocorrelation factor — how many draws this sampler needs to produce one draw’s worth of genuinely new information. R̂ buries it under a division by \(n\) and a square root, which is exactly why 1.00 and 1.02 look like neighbours.

R̂ is relative to how long you ran. Since \(\hat R \approx 1 + (\tau - 1)/2n\), doubling the draws roughly halves \(\hat R - 1\) with no improvement in mixing whatsoever — visible in the table above, where the same threshold buys wildly different amounts of autocorrelation at different \(n\). It is why effective sample size sits beside R̂ in every table on this page: R̂ alone can be bought with runtime.

1.00 is not the floor, and the version reported here is stricter
