Height buys something real, at two to three standard errors — but not a settled shape

Six candidate height shapes were compared by LOO — leave-one-out cross-validation, which scores a model by how well it predicts each observation with that observation held out. The score is elpd (expected log pointwise predictive density): the model’s total log-probability for data it did not see, so higher is better and only differences between models carry meaning.

Height matters — at two to three standard errors, which is a real effect and not an overwhelming one Real, small

Among climbers with three or more sends, adding a height term is worth about +25 ± 9 elpd in the original version and +17 to +22 ± 8 once the per-climber offsets are integrated out. That is the column where two identical refits agree almost exactly (+25.4 and +25.0), which is what makes it trustworthy. It is not the overwhelming result an unlabelled “25 points” would suggest.

Which shape height takes is not settled, and the shape used elsewhere is the weakest of them Open

The saturating curve sits near the bottom in the original version and at the top once the offsets are integrated out. That swing is about 1.5× the error on it, so the two are not in contradiction — they simply do not pin the shape down. Any claim of a specific functional form is reading past what the data supports.

More awkwardly: the curved, gender-split form used elsewhere in this work fails to beat no height term at all in three of the four columns once the offsets are integrated out, and its best column is well inside the error. Both versions agree it belongs near the bottom, so this is not an artefact of the change. The headline model should most likely be a straight line.

The comparison, scored against a fixed reference rather than a moving one

Scoring every model against whichever model won is a poor yardstick: the winner is picked using the same data it is then scored on, so its score is flattered, and the winner changes from column to column so the zero point moves. Scoring against a fixed reference — the model with no height term at all — fixes both, and asks the question the comparison exists to ask.

A gap needs an error bar, and these never had one. The two models being compared score the same rows, and their per-row scores are strongly correlated — both find the same climbers hard to predict. Subtracting row by row before adding up cancels that shared difficulty, and the uncertainty collapses: from about ±145 on either model’s total to ±8–19 on the difference between them. That smaller number is the one that says whether a gap is real.
