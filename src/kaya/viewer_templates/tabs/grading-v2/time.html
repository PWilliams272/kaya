How Climbers Improve Over Time

This section comes early because its answer is an input to the model that follows, not a commentary on it. The model treats a climber as a fixed quantity. They are not. Its ceiling for climber \(u\) at gym \(g\) is

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g$$

and there is no \(t\) anywhere in it. So if someone logged at one gym in 2022 and another in 2025, three years of getting better is charged to the second gym. The model has no other place to put it. A gym whose climbers reached it late in their own careers will look stiffer than it is; one they passed through early will look softer.

This section asks two questions in order, because the second cannot be answered without the first: how fast do climbers actually improve, and is the gym result contaminated?

How fast do climbers advance?

How this is measured

Each climber’s sends are bucketed into 90-day windows. A window with at least 5 sends gets a level: the hardest grade sent in it. Windowed, not cumulative — a running max-so-far only ever goes up, which would manufacture improvement out of noise. A windowed max can fall.

Consecutive windows up to 15 months apart then give a rate in grades per year. Both estimators use the same windows and the same rate; they differ only in which window decides the grade bin.

The obvious way to measure this is to take each climber’s level now, their level later, and bin the difference by where they started:

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\ell_{2} - \ell_{1}}{\Delta t}\ ,\qquad \text{binned by }\ \ell_{1}$$

This is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that produces a very convincing answer. Because \(\ell_1\) is a maximum over a handful of sends, a lucky window inflates it — and the next window, being ordinary, looks like a decline. The same noise that puts a climber in a high bin pushes their measured change downward. The estimator tilts the curve by construction.

How you can tell it is broken. The naive estimator says V11 climbers lose 3.6 grades a year. Nobody does that. When an estimator returns a physically impossible number at one end, the whole curve is suspect — not just that bin.

The fix: stop asking one measurement to do two jobs

Look at what \(\ell_1\) is being used for above. It decides which grade bin the climber goes in, and it is also the starting point that gets subtracted. One measurement, two jobs — and a single piece of luck hits both at once, in opposite directions:

  • A climber has a great week, so \(\ell_1\) comes out a grade too high.
  • Job one files them a grade too high.
  • Job two makes \(\ell_2 - \ell_1\) come out a grade too low.

So the top bins are systematically stocked with climbers whose starting point was inflated, and an inflated starting point mechanically produces a negative change. You get “strong climbers decline” out of data where nobody’s ability moved at all.

So hand job one to a different window. Use three windows instead of two: \(w_0\) only decides the bin, and the change is measured between \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) — windows \(w_0\) had nothing to do with.

$$\text{rate} = \frac{\ell_{w_2} - \ell_{w_1}}{\Delta t}\ ,\qquad \text{binned by }\ \ell_{w_0}$$

Now the luck that landed someone in the V8 bin happened in a window that is not part of the subtraction. It cannot tilt the measured change either way. The wire between the two errors is cut.

The same trap outside climbing. Suppose you want to know whether shorter children grow faster, and your tape measure is sloppy. If one measurement both decides who counts as short and serves as the baseline you subtract, then every child accidentally measured too short is filed as short and appears to grow extra. You will “discover” that short children grow faster — with a clean, monotone curve — even if every child grows at exactly the same rate. Measure on Monday to decide who is short, measure growth from Tuesday to Friday, and Monday’s slop cannot manufacture Tuesday-to-Friday growth.

Two costs, and the important one runs in the safe direction. Every climber now needs three good windows instead of two, so 20,756 triples replace 30,132 pairs. And because the bin label \(\ell_{w_0}\) is itself noisy, climbers get sorted into slightly wrong bins, which flattens any real relationship. So the de-biased slope is more likely an understatement of the grade dependence than an overstatement — which is the direction to err in when the claim being made is that advancement slows down.

A small second bug: dividing by a wobbly number

Nothing above pins down \(\Delta t\). \(w_2\) is just “the next window with enough sends”, so every climber contributes a change measured over their own gap — median 3 months, but some barely over one. And \(\ell\) is a max over integer grades, so nearly every gap produces a change of exactly \(0\) or \(\pm 1\). Divide a \(+1\) by a gap of \(0.09\) years and that climber enters the average at +11 grades a year.

$$\overline{\left(\frac{\Delta \ell}{\Delta t}\right)} \;\neq\; \frac{\overline{\Delta \ell}}{\overline{\Delta t}}$$

The fix is to divide once, at the end: average the changes, average the elapsed times, then take the ratio. It is worth doing and it is nearly free — but it is worth being clear that it barely moves the answer. The floor at \(\Delta t > 0.08\) years was already excluding the worst offenders, and the correction turns out to be around 0.05 grades a year. Which raises the real question, since the choice of measurement window moves the low grades by four times that much.

The real problem: how long do you watch?

Nothing so far says how long \(\Delta t\) should be, and that turns out to matter far more than the arithmetic. Hold everything else fixed and change only the watching period:

At V1 the answer moves from +0.88 grades a year to +0.22 depending on nothing but how long you look. The high bins barely move at all. A number that swings by a factor of four on an arbitrary choice is not a measurement of anything, so the question is which one — if either — is right.

Neither. A rate has to compound, and this one does not. Saying V1 climbers improve at 0.88 grades a year is a prediction: watch for a year and they should gain 0.88 grades. They gain 0.22, which is 4.8 standard errors away. The V1 group gains about a fifth of a grade after three months — and still about a fifth of a grade after twelve. That is a one-time step, not a rate, and dividing a step by the window you happened to pick just reports your choice back to you. Divide it by 0.25 and it reads +0.88; divide the same step by 1.0 and it reads +0.22.

So stop picking a window. Use them all: a genuine rate means the gain grows in proportion to elapsed time, so fit that line through the origin across all six horizons at once, and let the fit report whether the proportionality actually holds.

$$\overline{\Delta \ell}(h) = f(v)\cdot h \ ,\qquad \hat f(v) = \frac{\sum_h w_h\, h\, \overline{\Delta \ell}(h)} {\sum_h w_h\, h^2}\ ,\qquad w_h = 1/\mathrm{SE}_h^2$$

This is the one estimator that can be wrong in a way you can see. If a bin were really a step rather than a rate, the points would not lie on a line through the origin and the \(\chi^2\) would say so. It comes out near one in almost every bin — including V1, once the inflated three-month point stops being taken on its own.

The bin labels are lying, and always toward the middle. \(\ell_{w_0}\) is itself a noisy max, so each bin is stocked with climbers whose real level sits closer to the population average. Measured at the start of the window, the “V1” bin averages V2.0 and the “V9” bin averages V8.6. The x-axis is compressed by about a grade at each end, so every slope below is an underestimate of the real grade dependence — the safe direction to err when the claim is that advancement slows down.

Pooled across the bulk of the data, that line is about as clean as this kind of measurement gets. V3–V8 together, the gain accumulates in proportion to elapsed time from three months out to two years — so for most climbers improvement really is a steady rate, and the curve below is about where you are rather than how long anyone looked.

Is the gym result contaminated?

Now the question that motivated it. For every \((u,g)\) row, take the date of that climber’s hardest send at that gym and centre it within the climber:

$$\tilde t_{u,g} = t_{u,g} - \bar t_u \qquad \text{where } \bar t_u = \frac{1}{|G_u|}\sum_{g' \in G_u} t_{u,g'}$$

Within-climber centring is what turns this from a description into a test. It does not ask when a gym’s climbers were active; it asks whether this gym’s row is late for this person — precisely the quantity an unmodelled improvement trend would smuggle into the gym correction. How this goes into the model

The obvious repair is one parameter: a population improvement rate \(\tau\) applied to the centred date.

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g + \tau\,\tilde t_{u,g}$$

One number, no new geometry, and it absorbs exactly the “this climber’s row here is two years later than their row there” effect. But the advancement curve above says a constant is the wrong shape. Improvement is grade-dependent — around a fifth of a grade a year at V3, essentially flat by V7 — so a single \(\tau\) would over-correct strong climbers and under-correct weak ones. The rate has to be a function of where the climber already is, and the section above is exactly that function. It is used directly, interpolating between the measured bins. A straight line through them is a fair description — the fitted curve runs close to \(0.34 - 0.035\,v\) — but interpolation costs nothing and does not have to be defended.

$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g + f\!\left(\bar m_u\right)\tilde t_{u,g}\ ,\qquad f = \text{interp}\big(\{(v,\ \hat f_v)\}\big)$$

The decisive choice is whether \(f\) is fitted or supplied. It is supplied — fixed at the measured values, contributing a known offset with no free parameter. The reason is that two different mechanisms produce the same correlation, and nothing in this data separates them:

  • Climbers improved between their two gyms. That is the curve measured above.
  • Gyms’ grading drifted — setters across the network got stiffer over the same period.

A fitted \(\tau\) would inflate to absorb whichever is larger and then charge all of it to climber improvement, soaking up real gym signal and biasing the corrections toward each other. A fixed offset can only remove the amount improvement actually justifies. It is the conservative direction, and the one that survives being questioned.

The tell that this matters. The observed date-to-correction slope is +0.55 grades per year, while measured advancement where the median climber sits is +0.10. If improvement were the whole story those two numbers would agree. They differ by roughly 5×, so most of the correlation is not climbers getting better — and a free \(\tau\) would land near that ratio and report a false explanation with confident-looking error bars.

Expect the correction to be small. The gyms span 0.90 years of centred date, so at the measured rate the offset moves the corrections by about 0.09 grades against an observed spread of 1.29 — a few percent. It is worth carrying because it is comparable to the gym-correction standard errors and can reorder gyms that sit close together, not because it rescues the headline. If it moved things by half a grade, that would be evidence the fixed curve is wrong rather than evidence something had been repaired.

The fixed version is also insensitive to binning. \(f\) runs from 0.25 at V3 to 0.04 at V9 — the whole span is a fifth of a grade per year, and across V4–V8, where the great majority of modelled climbers sit, it varies by half that. Misfiling someone by a grade changes their offset by hundredths, which matters because the bin labels are known to be off by about that much.

Still open, in order.

1. Carry the date through. An observation is a (climber, gym) pair — the hardest send there — so the date needed is that send’s, centred within climber. prepare_base_data currently reads the send date, uses it for visit counts, and drops it.

2. Report the shift, not a new headline. Keep the no-offset fit as the comparison arm and quote how far the corrections move.

3. Fit \(\tau\) once, as a diagnostic. Not to ship — to measure. If the fitted rate lands near the ratio in the box above rather than near the measured curve, that is evidence for gym-side grading drift, which deserves its own section rather than being quietly corrected away.
