The Model, Formally

An item-response-theory model: climbs are test questions, climbers are test-takers, a send is a correct answer. The wrinkle is that Kaya records only sends — never failed attempts — so the standard IRT likelihood is not identified. Everything below follows from working around that one missing piece of data.

Ability

Each climber \(u\) has a latent ability on the grade scale:

$$\text{ability}_u = \beta_0 + \mathbf{x}_u^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \sigma_{\text{user}}\,\tilde\epsilon_u, \qquad \tilde\epsilon_u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

where \(\mathbf{x}_u\) is the covariate row for that climber — gender, height terms, ape-index terms, and two missingness indicators — each column centred to mean zero, which makes \(\beta_0\) orthogonal to every slope.

Height and ape index

Height and ape index are near-independent here (\(r = +0.137\)), so their shapes are specified separately. \(\tilde h\) and \(\tilde a\) are centred at the median and scaled to standard deviations.

The gender-interaction form is easiest to read as what it actually is — one curve per gender, blended. \(G\) is the probability the climber is female, so \(1-G\) and \(G\) are the weights:

$$f_{\text{height}}(\tilde h,\ G) \;=\; \underbrace{(1-G)\left(\gamma_1^{M}\tilde h + \gamma_2^{M}\tilde h^{2}\right)}_{\text{male users}} \;+\; \underbrace{G\left(\gamma_1^{F}\tilde h + \gamma_2^{F}\tilde h^{2}\right)}_{\text{female users}}$$

Note that \(G\) is a probability, not a hard label. A climber whose name is 70% female contributes 0.7 of a row to the female curve and 0.3 to the male one, which is how uncertainty about the name propagates into the height estimate instead of being silently rounded away.

What is actually fitted. The sampler does not see \(\gamma^{M}\) and \(\gamma^{F}\) as two free pairs. It fits a baseline and a difference — \(\gamma^{M}_{k}=\gamma_k\) and \(\gamma^{F}_{k}=\gamma_k+\gamma_k^{\times}\) — which is the same function, but lets the prior sit on the thing under test. A tight \(\gamma_k^{\times}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\,\text{small})\) shrinks the two curves toward each other, so a gender difference has to be earned from the data rather than appearing by default. Fitting two independent pairs would impose no such discipline. The male/female form above is the same model, written for reading.
$$f_{\text{ape}}(\tilde a) \;=\; \delta_1\tilde a + \delta_2\tilde a^{2}$$

Six candidate height shapes are compared by LOO — leave-one-out cross-validation — on identical data: the gender-blended quadratic above, a plain quadratic, linear, zero, a monotone saturating form — "reach helps until you have enough of it, then stops paying" — and a vertex quadratic that estimates the best height directly:

$$f_{\text{sat}}(\tilde h) \;=\; A\,\operatorname{logistic}\!\left(\frac{\tilde h - h_0}{s}\right) \qquad f_{\text{vtx}}(\tilde h) \;=\; -\kappa_h\left(\tilde h - p\right)^{2}$$

Every symbol, defined

Ability, grades, and gym corrections all live on the same scale — V-grades — which is what makes a correction of “+0.4” readable as “four tenths of a grade stiffer.” Every symbol above and below is defined in the Symbols panel, docked to the right so it stays in view while you read. The six height forms tested

The point of comparing these is that they encode genuinely different claims about the world, not just different numbers of parameters. All are compared by LOO (leave-one-out cross-validation) on identical data.

Play with the parameters

One card per form: the equation, the shape it produces, and a slider for every parameter in it. The point is to see what each form can and cannot say — no setting of the linear sliders will produce a peak, and no setting of the saturating sliders will produce a decline. Where a fit has landed, the sliders start at the fitted values; the rest start at illustrative ones and are labelled as such. Shaded bands mark where male and female users actually sit, which is why the same curve can look like “no effect” for one group and a strong effect for the other.

In every card the left band is where female users sit (64.2 ± 2.8 in) and the right band is male users (69.2 ± 3.3 in), each one standard deviation wide. All six cards share a vertical scale so their shapes are directly comparable; drag a slider to an extreme and the axis grows rather than clipping the curve.

Does centring height at the median force the peak to sit there? No — worth being explicit about, because it looks like it should. Centring only fixes where \(\tilde h=0\) falls; it says nothing about where the curve turns over. For \(f(\tilde h)=\gamma_1\tilde h+\gamma_2\tilde h^2\) the vertex sits at \(\tilde h^{*}=-\gamma_1/2\gamma_2\), which the data are free to put anywhere — v1’s own fitted coefficients implied a peak roughly 10 inches below the median.

What centring does change is the implied prior on that peak. A ratio of two normals is Cauchy-like — heavy-tailed and piled up near zero — so the plain quadratic quietly favours a peak near the median, and gives a badly-behaved interval whenever \(\gamma_2\) is close to zero. The vertex quadratic makes the peak \(p\) a parameter with a prior of its own, so it reports a credible interval directly instead of propagating error through a division. Both forms are fitted; if they disagree about where the best height is, that disagreement is itself the finding.

Gym correction

The model is identified only up to an additive shift between ability and correction, so corrections are constrained to sum to zero exactly — "relative to the average gym" by construction:

$$\text{gym}_g = \sigma_{\text{gym}}\,\tilde\delta_g, \qquad \tilde\delta \sim \text{ZeroSumNormal}(0,1)$$
$$C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g$$

The gap, and the likelihood

Nobody sends their true limit every session, so the logged max sits at or below the ceiling by a non-negative gap, whose rate rises with how many chances the climber has had to find their limit:

$$\log \lambda_{u,g} = \log\lambda_0 + \kappa\,\tilde n_{u,g} + \rho\,\tilde r_u$$
$$\text{gap}_{u,g}\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda_{u,g}), \qquad m_{u,g} = C_{u,g} - \text{gap}_{u,g} + \varepsilon,\ \ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{link}})$$

A Normal minus an Exponential is an Exponentially Modified Gaussian, so the gap integrates out in closed form and never has to be sampled:

$$-m_{u,g} \;\sim\; \text{ExGaussian}\!\left(-C_{u,g},\ \sigma_{\text{link}},\ 1/\lambda_{u,g}\right)$$

That removes one latent variable per observation — 32,501 of them on this network — and turns the likelihood into an ordinary observed node, so LOO (leave-one-out cross-validation) and posterior predictive checks work natively. Verified against numerical integration of the original formulation to 1×10−8.
