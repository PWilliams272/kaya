Everything that could be fooling us

The headline number is one grading correction per gym. Anything that makes two climbers’ logged grades differ for a reason other than the gym’s grading is a threat to it, and the model has nowhere to put what it does not represent — an effect it cannot express does not vanish, it gets absorbed into the nearest parameter that can move, which is usually the gym correction itself.

So this is the full list, whether or not anything is done about each one. Four of these were found by accident while looking for something else, which is the argument for enumerating them: a factor that is deliberately ignored is a decision; a factor nobody wrote down is a bug waiting for someone else to find. Each entry states the empirical evidence, the competing mental models, why it would contaminate a correction, how big it is in grades, and what handling it would take.

The scale everything is quoted against. The gym corrections themselves span 1.29 grades end to end, with a standard deviation of 0.284. A factor worth 0.284 grades is as large as the entire signal; one worth 0.03 is noise against it. Sizes below are meaningless without that comparison, so it is repeated on every row.

The ledger, ranked by size

In the model means the fitted likelihood represents it today. Measured, not modelled means the effect has been quantified without a sampler and confirmed real, but the model has no parameter for it — so it is currently being absorbed somewhere else. Open means not yet quantified.

Ceiling attainment — how many days they climbed there

The largest single correction in the model, and it is already in it In the model

A climber with three days at a gym has almost certainly not found their limit there. One with two hundred days has. The model must not read those two numbers as equally informative about ability, or every gym that people visit occasionally looks stiff.

Evidence. Exposure is wildly uneven and it is uneven within the same person: 41% of (climber, gym) cells have five days or fewer while 8.5% have a hundred or more, and for a typical climber who logs at several gyms the ratio between their most-visited and least-visited gym is 7.5× (60× at the 90th percentile).

Mental models. Either the hardest logged grade is the climber’s ceiling at that gym — in which case nothing needs doing — or it is a sample from below the ceiling whose shortfall shrinks the more they climb. The second is obviously right and the only question was its shape.

How it is handled. The likelihood is not Gaussian, it is ex-Gaussian: a Normal plus an Exponential. The Exponential term is the shortfall below the ceiling, and its mean shrinks with exposure.

$$m_{u,g} = C_{u,g} - \mathrm{Exp}(\nu_{u,g}) + \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)$$
$$\nu_{u,g} = \exp\!\left(-\left[\lambda_0 + \kappa\, n^{\text{visits}}_{u,g} + \rho\, r_u\right]\right)$$

More days → larger bracket → smaller \(\nu\) → smaller assumed shortfall. Fitted on v10_lin_marg: \(\lambda_0=\)0.081, \(\kappa=\)0.277.

Climber advancement — they got better in between

Measured, then put in — as a fixed offset, on 2026-08-07 In the model

Until 2026-08-07 there was no \(t\) anywhere in \(C_{u,g}\): a climber was one number for all time, so when someone logged at one gym two years ago and another last month, the improvement in between had nowhere to go except the second gym’s correction. Each ceiling now carries a known, fixed shift for when that send happened.

Evidence. Climbers advance fast when new and barely at all near their limit — about +0.28 grades/year at V1 falling to +0.04 at V9, fitted across every watching period at once rather than read off a single window. And the sends being compared really are far apart in time: the median gap between a climber’s two hardest sends at two gyms is 0.31 years, with 16% more than a year apart.

Mental models. The naive one — take each climber’s first and last max and divide by the elapsed time — is broken, and instructively so: it claims V11 climbers lose a grade a year. Being sorted into the top bin requires a high first reading and a high reading is partly luck, so the next reading is lower regardless. The rate above is fitted across all windows precisely to remove that sorting.

Why it matters — and the part that is easy to get wrong. A time gap only distorts a gym’s correction if that gym is systematically the later one. Where climbers visit two gyms in no particular order the effect cancels. So the right measurement is the mean gap per gym pair, not the average gap overall — and it is much smaller than the raw gap suggests, because people who climb at two gyms tend to climb at both in the same era.

How much. Across 80 gym pairs sharing at least 25 climbers, the median pair carries 0.043 grades of advancement misread as grading — small. But the worst carries 0.279, which is 98% of the whole standard deviation of the corrections. It is the tail that justifies fixing this, not the average.

Converted at a flat 0.185 grades per year of separation, the empirical average across abilities. The true rate falls with grade, so this understates the effect for weak climbers and overstates it for strong ones.

Where these dates come from. Every figure above is computed on 100% of the modelled (climber, gym) pairs (20,014 of 20,014, spanning 2020-08-25 to 2026-07-26). The date used is max_send_date — the date of the very send the model turns into an observation, carried through the aggregation rather than joined back on afterwards, so there is no second source that can fall out of step.

That was not true until 2026-08-07. The aggregate every fit reads kept dates only as a visit count, which is why the model has no t in it at all, and no script in the repo rebuilt that aggregate — so the single input the project rests on had no reproducible provenance. scripts/build_base_snapshot.py now writes it, and prepare_base_data carries three date columns through. The rebuild reproduced the previous snapshot row for row, so nothing about the fitted results changed — only what is now answerable.

How it is handled. Cheaply, and — unlike gym drift — without rebuilding the dataset. Each (climber, gym) row already comes from one hardest send with one date; the aggregation used to discard it. It now carries it through, and each ceiling gets a fixed offset:

$$C_{u,g} \;\rightarrow\; C_{u,g} + r(v_u)\,\bigl(t_{u,g} - \bar{t}_u\bigr), \qquad r(v) = 0.338 - 0.035\,v$$

Fixed, never fitted. A free advancement parameter would absorb 3.4× its true value — the raw correction-versus-date slope in this data is 0.827 grades/year against a real improvement rate near 0.24 — and would drag every gym correction with it. That is the one firm decision in this section.

What it actually did. Recovering the gym corrections by least squares, with and without the offset — the same two-way decomposition the model performs, minus the priors, so it runs in seconds rather than four hours:

Five percent of the spread that had been read as gyms grading differently was climbers improving. That the spread shrinks is not by itself evidence — subtracting any climber-centred vector removes some variance — so the same measurement was repeated 200 times with each climber’s shifts randomly reassigned among their own gyms. That preserves every magnitude and destroys only the link between a gym and when the climber was there. Shuffled, the spread stays at 0.4008 ± 0.0014; the real assignment reaches 0.3806, z = +13.9, \(p < 0.0001\). The timing carries real information.

Least squares has no shortfall term and no uncertainties, so this says the correction is worth fitting, not what it is worth. The paired fits — identical but for the offset and the seed — are queued. scripts/probe_advancement.py reproduces the table; src/kaya/advancement.py is the offset itself.

Gym grading drift — the gym itself changed

The largest effect in the data, and nothing was looking for it Measured, not modelled

A gym’s correction is one number for all time, exactly as a climber’s ability is. Setters change, standards move, a wall gets rebuilt.

Evidence. Same identity as everything else here: for a climber at two gyms the difference of their two levels cancels the climber entirely, so regressing that difference on calendar date isolates the gyms’ relative movement. Across 121 gym pairs the scatter is far beyond noise — \(I^2=\)68% of it is real — giving τ = 0.163 grades per year, against a simulated detection floor of 0.071. Two independent routes agree: a per-gym solve gives 0.105 where the pooled route predicts 0.115.

Why it matters, and why it matters less than it looks. Over the 5.9-year span of the data that rate accumulates to 0.62 grades — half the entire spread the corrections are trying to measure. But between gyms only the difference in their mean dates matters, and those differ by just 0.57 years, so the distortion actually injected into the correction comparison is 0.063 grades. Real, and an order of magnitude below the headline.

An honest limit. This design sees only relative drift. If every gym in the network stiffened together, the paired difference would cancel it exactly and this measurement would read zero. Global drift is not ruled out — it is unmeasurable with this data.

How to handle it. Give the correction a time argument. This one does need the dataset rebuilt to one row per (climber, gym, 90-day window), because a slope needs more than one time point per gym.

$$\gamma_g \;\rightarrow\; \gamma_g + \delta_g\,\bigl(t - \bar{t}\bigr), \qquad \delta_g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau_\delta),\;\; \textstyle\sum_g \delta_g = 0$$

Zero-sum on \(\delta_g\) for the same reason as on \(\gamma_g\): a common drift shared by every gym is absorbed into the overall time trend and is not identifiable separately.

Grade compression — one number per gym is not enough

A gym’s correction depends on how strong the climber is Measured, not modelled

The model assumes a gym is equally stiff for a V2 climber and a V9 climber. It is not. Some gyms bunch their top grades together; others stretch them out.

Evidence. The same paired difference, regressed on the climber’s ability instead of the date — the identical trick on a perpendicular axis. Across 109 gym pairs, \(I^2=\)60% of the scatter is real, giving τ = 0.074 grades of correction change per grade of ability, against a detection floor of 0.039. It replicates artifact-free at 0.0753 when ability is measured from gyms outside the pair being compared, which removes the shared-noise objection. There is also real curvature — τ = 0.023, \(I^2=\)53% — so the right shape is a per-gym quadratic, not a line.

How much. Over the V3 to V8 range where the data actually lives, that is 0.52 grades of difference between how a gym treats its weak and strong climbers — 40% of the full spread of the corrections.

The constraint that shapes any fix. The correction must depend on the climber’s latent ability, never their observed grade at that gym. Using the outcome to select its own correction is circular and will manufacture a slope from noise alone. And only relative compression is identifiable: a slope shared by every gym is absorbed into a rescaled ability axis and cancels in the paired differences, so the per-gym slopes need a zero-sum constraint just as the intercepts do.

$$\gamma_g \;\rightarrow\; a_g + b_g\bigl(C_u - \bar{C}\bigr) + c_g\bigl(C_u - \bar{C}\bigr)^2, \qquad \textstyle\sum_g b_g = \sum_g c_g = 0$$

Height and arm span — and why the shape matters

Height belongs; the gender difference lives in the curvature, not the slope In the model

If tall climbers cluster at one gym and the model has the wrong shape for height, the residual lands in that gym’s correction.

Evidence. Tested without a sampler, on 8,695 climbers, by removing each gym’s grading with a two-way least-squares solve and then cross-validating candidate height forms over climbers. Repeated across 25 independent fold assignments, because a verdict that moves with the shuffle is a property of the shuffle.

Positive σ favours the richer form; 2σ is the bar. quadratic_x_gender wins on 25/25 seeds.

The mechanism, which explains every row of that table. The two curvatures have opposite signs. So a form that makes both genders share one bend averages them toward zero and measures nothing, and a form with no bend at all leaves the gender interaction nothing to act on — which is exactly why adding gender to a straight line buys nothing while adding it to a curve clears the bar.

The form the data prefers is the form the sampler cannot reach Unresolved tension

The cross-validation above needs no sampler, and it is unambiguous. The Bayesian fit of that same form is not: on identical settings to its siblings it returned R-hat 1.53 against a bar of 1.01, an effective sample size of 7 against a floor of 400, and 1,500 divergent transitions — a quarter of all draws. Its cross-validation score is therefore withheld rather than reported, because scoring chains that never mixed measures the chains, not the model.

Notably this is not the failure that plagued the earlier fits. Those saturated the sampler’s trajectory limit on every iteration; this one sits at 0% of the limit with a mean depth of 4. The geometry is pathological in a different way, and the likeliest reason is thin support: the four height parameters include two that act only on the 29.0% of climbers coded female, so the interaction terms are being asked for more than the data holds. Two independent methods disagree about the same model, and that is stated rather than resolved by picking the one with the more convenient answer.

Gender coding — inferred from a first name

Not observed, estimated — and carried as a probability rather than a guess Partly handled

Evidence and handling. Kaya does not record gender, so it is inferred from first name and refined by height. Rather than force a coin flip on every ambiguous name, the model carries \(w^{\text{female}}_u \in [0,1]\) — a probability, not a flag. On top of that, every result on this page uses the confident-names-only subset, which is why the modelled population is already almost cleanly split: 69.6% code confidently male, 29.0% confidently female, and only 1.5% sit in between. The ambiguous names were excluded up front rather than guessed at — the residual uncertainty shown here is what survives that filter, not the raw difficulty of the problem.

Why it matters. Misclassification attenuates any gender effect toward zero. Since the height finding is entirely a gender-interaction finding, that attenuation acts directly on the one result it would most easily erase — which is the argument for the confident-only subset despite the sample cost.

Grade quantization — V-grades are integers

A ceiling is only ever observed to the nearest whole grade Partly handled

Evidence. Only 3.7% of the network’s 268,442 dated sends survive into the model — one hardest climb per (climber, gym) and the rest discarded. And that surviving number is fragile: 50.5% of cells sent their maximum exactly once, which could be a soft climb or a lucky day, while 3.0% sent it ten or more times and are clearly plateaued there. Those two cases are currently treated as equally reliable evidence of ability.

How it is handled, and the gap. The ex-Gaussian shortfall absorbs the downward bias, and \(\sigma\) absorbs the rounding. But n_at_max — how many times they repeated their hardest grade, a far more direct signal of “have they found their ceiling” than visit count — exists in the data and is wired into the PyMC model behind a flag, yet is absent from the marginalized likelihood the current fits actually sample. It is available signal being left on the floor.

Selection — who logs, and when

The leading remaining explanation for the one confound that has not closed Open — not quantified

The evidence that something is missing. Gyms whose data is more recent get stiffer corrections, at r = +0.60. Three candidate explanations have been tested and none closes it. Climber advancement accounts for about 8%. Relative gym drift accounts for none — and fails in the informative direction: removing accumulated drift makes the relationship stronger (slope 0.809 → 0.955), which rules it out rather than leaving it open. Brand composition explains part: within a single operating company the correlation falls to +0.34.

The mental model that has not been tested. Who starts logging, when, and at which gym is not random. A gym that opened recently draws a different population than one with a decade of regulars — newer app adopters, different ability mix, different logging habits. None of that is in the model and none of it has been measured. This is the largest open question on the page and it is stated rather than smoothed over.
