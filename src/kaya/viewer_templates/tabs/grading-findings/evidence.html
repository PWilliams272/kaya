Why believe any of it

A fitted model always produces numbers. What decides whether they mean anything is a short list of checks — whether the sampler actually explored the distribution, whether independent runs agree, and whether the parameters are separately identified or merely trading off against each other.

The model comparison, with its convergence gate

Every candidate, its held-out score, and whether the fit that produced it converged. The gate travels with the result on purpose: a score from a fit whose chains disagreed is not a measurement, and the most common way to mislead yourself here is to quote one anyway.

Where each parameter landed

Posterior distributions for the fitted parameters. Width is uncertainty; a distribution straddling zero means the data did not determine the sign.

Did the chains agree?

Four chains start from different random points. If they all explored the same distribution, the variation between them matches the variation within each one — that ratio is R̂ (R-hat), and 1.00 is perfect agreement, lower is better. The companion number is ESS (effective sample size): consecutive draws are correlated, so 2,000 draws are not worth 2,000 independent ones, and ESS is how many they are worth. Higher is better; 400 is the usual floor.

What the sampler did

The honest summary. This model samples hard. There are no divergences — the sampler never fell off the surface — but it reaches its maximum trajectory length on essentially every iteration, and R̂ on the worst parameters sits above the 1.01 threshold rather than below it. That describes a posterior which is extremely elongated rather than broken: the cost is precision and runtime, not correctness. Two fixes are on the machine as this is written — a much longer warm-up, and rotating the covariate columns so they stop trading off against each other.
