The model, in five equations

Every symbol below is defined with its units in the Symbols panel on the right — hover an equation to highlight the ones it uses.

1. Ability lives on the grade scale

Each climber \(u\) gets a latent ability: a baseline \(\beta_0\) (the grade an average climber sends at an average gym), a body-covariate term \(\mathbf{x}_u\) carrying gender, height, arm span and two missingness flags, and a personal offset scaled by \(\sigma_{\text{user}}\), the spread of natural ability between climbers.

$$\text{ability}_u = \beta_0 + \mathbf{x}_u^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \sigma_{\text{user}}\,\tilde\epsilon_u, \qquad \tilde\epsilon_u \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

2. Gym corrections are forced to sum to zero

Ability and correction are identified only up to an additive shift — add a grade to every climber and subtract one from every gym and the data look identical. Pinning the corrections to sum to zero exactly removes that freedom, and makes “relative to the average gym” true by construction rather than by convention. A climber’s ceiling at a gym is then the two added.

$$\text{gym}_g = \sigma_{\text{gym}}\,\tilde\delta_g, \qquad \tilde\delta \sim \text{ZeroSumNormal}(0,1) \qquad C_{u,g} = \text{ability}_u + \text{gym}_g$$

3. Nobody sends their limit every session

This is the part that makes the problem hard. Kaya records sends, not attempts, so the hardest grade someone logged is not their ceiling — it sits below it by a non-negative gap. Someone who visited twice has had far less chance to find their limit than someone who visited fifty times, so the gap shrinks as \(\tilde n_{u,g}\), their standardised visit count, rises.

$$\log \lambda_{u,g} = \log\lambda_0 + \kappa\,\tilde n_{u,g} + \rho\,\tilde r_u$$
$$\text{gap}_{u,g}\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda_{u,g}), \qquad m_{u,g} = C_{u,g} - \text{gap}_{u,g} + \varepsilon,\ \ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{link}})$$

A Normal minus an Exponential is an Exponentially Modified Gaussian, so the gap integrates out in closed form and is never sampled — removing 32,501 latent variables and turning the likelihood into an ordinary observed node.

$$-m_{u,g} \;\sim\; \text{ExGaussian}\!\left(-C_{u,g},\ \sigma_{\text{link}},\ 1/\lambda_{u,g}\right)$$

The sliders below move the gap distribution. The point to take away is that the observed max is a biased view of the ceiling, and the bias depends on how often someone climbed.

4. Height enters as one curve per gender, blended by probability

\(G\) is the probability the climber is female — a probability, not a label. A climber whose name is 70% female contributes 0.7 of a row to the female curve and 0.3 to the male one, so uncertainty about the name propagates into the estimate instead of being silently rounded away.

$$f_{\text{height}}(\tilde h,\ G) \;=\; \underbrace{(1-G)\left(\gamma_1^{M}\tilde h + \gamma_2^{M}\tilde h^{2}\right)}_{\text{male users}} \;+\; \underbrace{G\left(\gamma_1^{F}\tilde h + \gamma_2^{F}\tilde h^{2}\right)}_{\text{female users}}$$
What is actually fitted. Not two free pairs, but a baseline and a difference: \(\gamma^{M}_{k}=\gamma_k\) and \(\gamma^{F}_{k}=\gamma_k+\gamma_k^{\times}\). Same function, but it puts the prior on the thing under test — a tight \(\gamma_k^{\times}\) shrinks the two curves toward each other, so a gender difference has to be earned from the data rather than appearing by default.

5. The shape of the height curve is itself a hypothesis

“Does height help?” is not one question. A straight line says every inch is worth the same; a curve says there is a best height and being taller past it hurts; a saturating form says reach helps until you have enough of it and then stops paying. These are different claims about the world, so each is fitted and scored rather than assumed. Drag the sliders — the useful thing is what each shape cannot say.
